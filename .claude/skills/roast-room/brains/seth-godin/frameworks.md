# Frameworks: Seth Godin

16 frameworks, grouped by the job they do (product, audience, permission and brand, launch and spread, persistence). "Sources" counts distinct sources in `sources.md`. Book names in italics show where he developed the idea; no book text was fetched, so every quote below comes from a talk, the 2024 interview, or the blog. These are thinking tools built from cases and observation, not tested models; score with them, then test with real sales.

## Product: is it remarkable?

### 1. The Purple Cow test *(Purple Cow, 2003)*
- **What it is:** People ignore the ordinary, so the thing that decides what spreads is whether the product itself is worth making a remark about. Remarkability lives in the product, its design and the problem it chooses to solve, not in the advertising.
- **Parts:** (a) Would the people it is for notice it among cows they have seen before (S001)? (b) Is it built into the product, like Dutch Boy's paint can or Silk's shelf placement (S001)? (c) Do we know in advance what we want them to say (S003)? (d) Does telling raise the teller's status (S003)?
- **When to use:** Any new SKU, line extension, pack redesign or launch brief.
- **How to score:** Ask five target customers what they would tell a friend. If the answer is a feature list or a price, it is a brown cow. Gimmicks, stunts and divisiveness fail the test (S003, S030).
- **Quote:** "They didn't just slap a new ad on the product; they changed what it meant to build a paint product." (S001)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S003, S010, S030, S054, S069)

### 2. Find the otaku
- **What it is:** Sell first to the people who are obsessed with the category, because they listen and they tell others; categories without an obsessed group are almost impossible to launch into.
- **Parts:** Identify the obsessed group; talk to them; make it easy for them to tell their friends; let it spread across the curve (S001). Movements start from a group that "already has a yearning" (S002).
- **When to use:** Choosing the launch audience and the first KOLs or seed customers.
- **How to score:** Is there a hot sauce otaku for this product, or is it mustard? If no group cares desperately, change the product or the audience before spending.
- **Quote:** "There's a hot sauce otaku, but there's no mustard otaku." (S001)
- **Sources:** 2 (S001, S002)

### 3. Very good is average; safe is risky
- **What it is:** In a crowded market, competent and inoffensive is the riskiest position because it is invisible.
- **Parts:** "The riskiest thing you can do now is be safe." (S001) Mediocre simply means average (S003). Grinding out "very good" does not produce a leap (S069).
- **When to use:** When a team defends a me-too product as "good quality" or safe for the mass market.
- **How to score:** Name the edge. If you cannot say what is different and who will care, you have chosen the middle.
- **Quote:** "Very good is boring. Very good is average." (S001)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S003, S069)

## Audience: who exactly is it for?

### 4. Smallest viable audience *(This Is Marketing, 2018)*
- **What it is:** Choose the smallest group that can sustain the work and that you can delight so much they recruit others. Small is a starting kernel for word of mouth, not a ceiling.
- **Parts:** Overwhelm a small audience (S004); pick "which no one" (S042); be specific so you get only the delighted and the rest (S040); small audiences raise response rates (S045). Choosing them sets language, price, problem and temperament (S003).
- **When to use:** Every launch, every SKU, every channel plan.
- **How to score:** Can you describe the audience by worldview and problem, not demographics alone, in one sentence, and name who it is not for?
- **Quote:** "By smallest possible I don't mean, too small. I mean the smallest number that eventually leads to the kernel of conversation that enables you to grow." (S004)
- **Sources:** 6 (S003, S004, S022, S040, S042, S045)

### 5. Who's it for? What's it for?
- **What it is:** Two questions to answer before a brief, a design, a budget or a meeting.
- **Parts:** Who's it for (a specific person with a worldview); what's it for (the change you seek to cause); would they miss it if you did not build it (S063).
- **When to use:** Briefing designers, agencies, KOLs; approving a product.
- **How to score:** If the team gives different answers, stop and align before anything is produced.
- **Quote:** "Who's it for? What's it for? If you can be really clear about what the work is for, then hire someone you trust and give them the freedom to find a way to cause that change to happen." (S033)
- **Sources:** 3 (S003, S033, S063)

### 6. Four strategic choices *(This Is Strategy, 2024)*
- **What it is:** Choose your customers, your competition, your source of validation and your distribution. Each choice decides the product and the life of the business.
- **Parts:** Customers (smallest viable audience); competition (who you will be compared to); validation (whose taste you are trying to match); distribution (where and how it is bought) (S003).
- **When to use:** Channel and launch decisions, especially "sell everywhere" plans.
- **How to score:** For each of the four, is it a deliberate choice or a default? A two-person team selling to every channel has not chosen (S003).
- **Quote:** "So again, these four things are choices, and if you're glossing over them it's because you're afraid." (S003)
- **Sources:** 1 (S003)

### 7. People like us do things like this *(This Is Marketing, 2018; Tribes, 2008)*
- **What it is:** Most choices are made in the context of culture. People adopt what people like them are doing, so the marketer's job is to make the product part of a group's identity.
- **Parts:** Adoption is solved by "what about the others?" (S007); switching means leaving an identity behind (S024); competition signals that people like us do this (S029); culture drives service and behavior (S026).
- **When to use:** Positioning, community, packaging cues, KOL selection, switching campaigns.
- **How to score:** Who is "us", and what visible behavior signals membership? If the product would embarrass the group, it fails.
- **Quote:** "Without a doubt, people like us do things like this, is the most powerful marketing mantra available. Make it true, then share the news." (S024)
- **Sources:** 4 (S007, S024, S026, S029)

### 8. Status roles
- **What it is:** People define themselves relatively, so purchases and recommendations move status up or down.
- **Parts:** Status is relative (S070); people talk about products when telling raises their standing (S003); adoption is about what the others do (S007).
- **When to use:** Premium lines, gifting, licensed character goods, referral design.
- **How to score:** Does owning, showing or recommending this raise the user's status with the people they care about?
- **Quote:** "We define ourselves in relative terms, not absolute ones." (S070)
- **Sources:** 3 (S003, S007, S070)

## Permission and brand

### 9. Permission: anticipated, personal, relevant *(Permission Marketing, 1999)*
- **What it is:** Earn the right to talk to people who want to hear from you, instead of stealing attention from strangers.
- **Parts:** Anticipated, personal, relevant (S052); connection is a form of permission (S011); interruption is cheap for the sender and costly for each receiver (S052); the alternative to the attention race is the race to the top (S046).
- **When to use:** CRM, LINE OA, chat broadcasts, follower lists, live-stream audiences, sampling.
- **How to score:** Would they miss the message if it stopped? Would they be annoyed if it doubled?
- **Quote:** "To deliver anticipated, personal and relevant messages to people who want to get them." (S046)
- **Sources:** 3 (S011, S046, S052)

### 10. A brand is a promise (and the miss-you test)
- **What it is:** A brand is the expectation people hold about you; it is built by making and keeping promises, not by logos.
- **Parts:** Promise and expectation (S003, S038); "would I miss you if you were gone" (S003, S035, S068); logo vs. brand test: if Nike opened a hotel we would know what it is like (S003); loyalty means paying extra (S003); distinctiveness visible in use (S014).
- **When to use:** Brand architecture, sub-brands (premium line), rebrands, loyalty schemes.
- **How to score:** Would customers pay extra to stick with us? If not, the brand has no value yet (S003).
- **Quote:** "Loyalty is would you pay extra to stick with this? And if you wouldn't pay extra, then the brand has no value." (S003)
- **Sources:** 5 (S003, S014, S035, S038, S068)

### 11. Direct vs. brand marketing
- **What it is:** Direct marketing is measured and pays for itself; brand marketing is long-term and aspirational. Know which you are doing and do not let one corrupt the other.
- **Parts:** Direct is outbound, measured, self-funding (S005); the web makes everyone a direct marketer, but the brands that matter were not built by direct response (S005); direct marketers care about percentage, not reach (S045); a brand ad that works for any company is not a good ad (S017).
- **When to use:** Splitting budget between performance ads and brand work.
- **How to score:** For each spend line, is it measured response or long-term promise? If brand, could a competitor's name be swapped in?
- **Quote:** "What you need to sacrifice to make the numbers work might be the very brand you seek to build." (S005)
- **Sources:** 3 (S005, S017, S045)

## Launch and spread

### 12. Crossing from early adopters (activation, not competition)
- **What it is:** Launch to the people who like to go first, delight them, and let them tell the early majority. Innovators usually face no-one, not a competitor.
- **Parts:** Early adopters ask "is it new?"; early majority ask "did it work?" (S053); people show up when ready, not at launch (S053); activation beats competition (S059); new categories need explaining first, and competitors help explain (S050).
- **When to use:** Launch timing, new-category products, launch-day expectations.
- **How to score:** Is the plan built for the first few hundred delighted users and their conversation, or for a launch-day spike?
- **Quote:** "People don’t show up when you launch.They show up when they’re ready." (S053)
- **Sources:** 3 (S050, S053, S059)

### 13. Tribes: lead and connect *(Tribes, 2008)*
- **What it is:** A tribe is a group connected to each other, an idea and a leader. You do not own it; you can serve and lead it for a while.
- **Parts:** Three questions: who are you upsetting, who are you connecting, who are you leading (S002); connect customers to each other, not only to you (S011); the tribe is not yours (S041).
- **When to use:** Community, fan clubs, licensed character fans, ambassador programs.
- **How to score:** Do members connect with each other because of us, and would the group miss us?
- **Quote:** "The first one is, who exactly are you upsetting? Because if you're not upsetting anyone, you're not changing the status quo." (S002)
- **Sources:** 3 (S002, S011, S041)

### 14. Race to the top vs. race to the bottom
- **What it is:** Competing on price or stolen attention is a race no one can win; racing to the top (better, more specific, a dollar more and worth it) creates more winners.
- **Parts:** Cheap is the last refuge (S018, S019, S037); a dollar more, spent on quality (S016); the attention race (S046); winning the bottom means staying there (S056).
- **When to use:** Discount pressure, cost-down proposals, platform price wars.
- **How to score:** If we cut price or cost here, what promise breaks and who notices?
- **Quote:** "It’s tempting to race to the bottom. The problem is that you might win, and then you’ll have to stay there." (S056)
- **Sources:** 6 (S016, S018, S019, S037, S046, S056)

## Persistence and testing

### 15. Dip vs. dead end *(The Dip, 2007)*
- **What it is:** Worthwhile projects have a Dip, a long hard stretch before mastery or traction, that must be survived; other projects are dead ends (the book calls them cul-de-sacs) that never get better. Persist in the first, quit the second, and decide the quit rule before starting. This is not "quit fast".
- **Parts:** Know the difference between a dip and a dead end (S015); decide how and when to quit before you begin (S060); do not flit, but do not defend a project that has gone sideways (S055); stick it out through the trough of skepticism (S061).
- **When to use:** Reviewing a struggling SKU, channel or brand after launch.
- **How to score:** Is there evidence the people it is for are getting more engaged over time? If yes, it may be a Dip. If engagement is flat and the promise is not landing, it may be a dead end.
- **Quote:** "Sometimes the marketer faces a dip that must be survived, but the 10x marketer is also engaged enough to know the difference between that dip and a dead end that has no hope." (S015)
- **Sources:** 4 (S015, S055, S060, S061)

### 16. Will you, not would you
- **What it is:** Validate with sales and interaction, not surveys of intent.
- **Parts:** "Would you" questions get politeness; "will you" gets truth (S032); the best way to find market appeal is to try to sell it (S013).
- **When to use:** Concept tests, pre-orders, pilot SKUs.
- **How to score:** Did real people pay or commit? Stated intent does not count.
- **Quote:** "You can do all the research in the world, but until you have the guts to make a sale, it's difficult to be certain of anything." (S032)
- **Sources:** 2 (S013, S032)
