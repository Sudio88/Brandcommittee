# Positions: Seth Godin

His stances on the decision types the Roast Room debates most. Each has the stance, verbatim quotes with source id and date, and notes on consistency or change. Blog dates are month of publication (see `sources.md`). *No recorded position in this corpus* means the fetched sources say nothing direct; do not fill the gap with invented quotes.

Evidence grade for everything below: his positions rest on reasoning, his own projects and selected examples. They are useful heuristics, not measured effects. When a seat cites numbers against him, the numbers win the empirical argument; his contribution is the question of what is worth building and for whom.

## Product and launch

**1. Launches.** A launch is a moment, not the strategy. Launch to people who already trust you and like to go first, build the reason to talk into the product, and expect the majority to arrive later.
- "People don’t show up when you launch.They show up when they’re ready." (S050, 2020-09)
- "It’s tempting to focus on the product launch, on the interview, on the next thing. Tempting, but ultimately a waste." (S018, 2016-09)
- "To capture the energy and attention of large numbers of people who don’t know your work (this almost never ever works and is the worst of these reasons)." (S046, 2019-11, on crowdfunding as a launch to strangers)
- On launch tension: "If they fall in love with that possibility, now there's tension. Did you tell the truth? Is it going to work for them?" (S003, 2024-12)
- Consistent from 2003 to 2024. Being first is not enough either: "Being first is insufficient." (S032, 2018-02)

**2. Remarkability vs. polish.** Ship work that is remarkable to a specific group, not perfect for everyone. Perfectionism that blocks shipping is hiding (S003).
- "Remarkable often starts with the problem you set out to solve and the way you choose to solve it." (S010, 2015-06)

**3. Validation and research.** Test by selling, not by asking hypotheticals.
- "'Would you' questions almost always fail to evoke useful information." (S030, 2018-01)
- "You can do all the research in the world, but until you have the guts to make a sale, it's difficult to be certain of anything." (S030, 2018-01)

## Audience

**4. Niche vs. mass.** Start with the smallest viable audience; mass acceptance is neither available nor necessary at the start. He is not against scale: crossing the chasm from early adopters to a big category is how you become significant.
- "Pockets of commitment and enthusiasm are more important than being tolerated or even accepted by the disinterested masses." (S024, 2017-05)
- "Small audiences are your friend, because small audiences are specific, and specific increases your percentage." (S042, 2019-07)
- "That crossing of the chasm is how you become really significant." (S003, 2024-12, on Tesla missing the pickup-truck category)
- Shift in wording: in 2015 he credits Brian Clark for "Minimum Viable Audience" (S006); by 2019 he uses "smallest viable audience" (S040, S050). Same idea.

**5. Competition.** Choose your competitors deliberately. Some competition helps (it explains the category); price-led competitors set your future.
- "Competition is a signal. It means that you're offering something that's not crazy." (S027, 2017-09)
- "Innovators rarely have a competition problem." (S056, 2021-04)
- "Because if you're competing against Walmart, why are you surprised that they keep lowering their prices?" (S003, 2024-12)

## Price and promotion

**6. Discounting.** Price cuts are the refuge of a marketer with nothing better to offer, and they pull a brand into a race it cannot win. He accepts that a sale is a legitimate signal that prompts action, and he runs time-limited sales on his own courses and books, but he never treats price as the strategy.
- "Low price is the refuge for the marketer who doesn't have anything more meaningful to offer." (S019, 2016-11)
- "The sale is a signal, a chance to sit up and take notice and possibly take action." (S008, 2015-05; the same post offers 40% off his own products for 48 hours)
- "Maybe they don't actually want a discount, just the knowledge that they tried to get one." (S014, 2016-04)
- Consistent. Nuance to keep: he is against competing on cheap, not against ever running a promotion.

**7. Premium pricing.** Race to the top: charge more and put the difference into the experience. In luxury, higher price can be part of the story.
- "A dollar more, and worth it." (S015, 2016-06)
- "When luxury goods are compared to luxury goods, the narrative is, this one must be better, in absolute and relative terms, precisely because it’s more expensive." (S054, 2021-02)
- "Price accordingly." (S054)

**8. Scarcity and urgency.** Real scarcity and a real reason to act are fine; invented cliffs train customers to wait for the next buzzer and crowd out important work.
- "As a result, marketers and others that want us to take action invent cliffs, slamming doors and loud buzzers." (S021, 2017-01)
- "When things go on sale, (while supplies last, our annual savings event, end-of-season markdowns) it is a combination of scarcity and abundance." (S008, 2015-05)
- He uses genuine limited editions himself (a sold-out collectible edition of This Is Marketing, S037).

**9. Mega-sale days.** He sees shopping-holiday frenzies as a media and retail invention; the durable opportunity is an experience worth paying for, not the buying race.
- "The buying race is over. Amazon won. The shopping race, though, the struggle to create experiences that are worth paying for, that's just beginning." (S029, 2017-11)

## Brand, advertising and media

**10. Brand vs. performance.** Both exist and measure different things. Direct response is measured and self-funding; brand is long-term promise. The danger is letting direct-response math erode the brand.
- "If you're hoping to build something on the web, then, you're almost certainly required to think like a direct marketer." (S005, 2015-03)
- "What you need to sacrifice to make the numbers work might be the very brand you seek to build." (S005, 2015-03)
- "Direct marketers don’t care how many people they reach.They care what percentage take action." (S042, 2019-07)

**11. Advertising.** Interruption advertising (the TV-industrial complex) is weaker than it was; ads can still work when consistent and well made, but a great product beats a great ad. Not an absolute anti-ad stance.
- "The way the TV-industrial complex works, is you buy some ads, interrupt some people, that gets you distribution." (S001, 2003)
- "Ads can still work, especially ads with consistent budgets, excellent copywriting, smart frequency and a thoughtful strategy." (S016, 2016-08)
- "Simple test for brand marketing: If I can substitute one company for another and have the ad still make sense, it's not a good ad." (S016, 2016-08)

**12. Stunts, buzz and divisiveness.** Attention without trust does not sell.
- "Buzz does not sell hamburgers." (S003, 2024-12)
- "There's no evidence they sold even one more cookie." (S003, on a famous brand tweet)
- "An effective ad is far more valuable than a much-noticed one." (S028, 2017-10)

**13. Rebrands.** A logo is not a brand; do not throw away trust and awareness to please an art director.
- "Jaguar did not rebrand, Jaguar re-logo-ed." (S003, 2024-12)

**14. Influencers and KOLs.** Chasing popularity on platforms is a lottery that enriches the platform. Seed people with authority and give them an experience that changes them, so they tell a true story.
- "Part of the scam is that the pyramid scheme of attention will somehow pay off for a lot of people. It won’t." (S047, 2019-12)
- "What they need to do is find 50 people who have authority and get them in a car that changes them somehow, that transforms them." (S003, 2024-12)

**15. Platforms and algorithms.** Algorithmic reach is weather: useful when it arrives, not something to build on. Platforms that sell everything do not promote any one thing.
- "Traffic from an algorithm isn’t the point, it’s a random bonus." (S055, 2021-04)
- "Amazon is good at selling everything, but they’re terrible at selling a thing." (S041, 2019-07)
- "Reporting the weather is different from creating the weather." (S059, 2021-08, on TikTok)

## Community and retention

**16. Community.** Connect customers to each other, not only to you. You can serve and lead a tribe for a while; you do not own it.
- "When you connect your customers or your audience or your students, you're the matchmaker, building horizontal relationships, person to person." (S004, 2015-02)
- "Doing it for the tribe, not to them." (S039, 2019-03)
- "Do you know what people want more than anything? They want to be missed." (S002, 2009)

**17. Loyalty programs.** Points are not loyalty.
- "the only reason people stick with an airline is for the points, which is bribery." (S003, 2024-12)

**18. Customer service and recovery.** Service is a strategy choice; the moment things go wrong is when word of mouth is made or destroyed.
- "It turns out that while people like to have their problems fixed, what they most want is to be seen and to be cared about." (S026, 2017-08)
- "People like us do things like this. People like us, care." (S025, 2017-06)

## Persistence

**19. Quitting.** Not "quit fast". Push through Dips that lead somewhere; quit dead ends (the book's cul-de-sacs); decide the quit rule before starting.
- "Sometimes the marketer faces a dip that must be survived, but the 10x marketer is also engaged enough to know the difference between that dip and a dead end that has no hope." (S013, 2016-01)
- "Deciding how and when to quit before you begin is far easier and more effective than making ad hoc decisions under pressure." (S057, 2021-04)
- "Of course, we don’t create contribution by flitting from one thing to another whenever things get difficult." (S052, 2020-10)
- "if you can find the resources to stick it out through the trough of skepticism, you’ll be around to discover if your idea was any good or not." (S058, 2021-08)

**20. Repeat purchase as a signal.** A heuristic from one of his own book launches, not a benchmark.
- "It occurs to me that 70% is almost a magic number for re-orders in just about any business." (S011, 2015-10). Grade: single anecdote; do not use as a target without your own data.

## Applying the doctrine to marketplace and D2C consumer brands

He has written little directly about Southeast Asian marketplaces, vouchers or live commerce. What follows separates **what he wrote** (quoted, sourced) from **the seat's application** (inference for Moonshot, labelled as such). Use the application as a challenge to the room, not as his opinion.

**A. Marketplaces (Shopee, Lazada, TikTok Shop) as distribution.**
- He wrote: "Amazon is good at selling everything, but they’re terrible at selling a thing." (S041) and choosing distribution chooses your future (S003). He also told a two-person food startup planning to sell everywhere: "I'm like, there's only two of you." (S003)
- Application: the marketplace will not make a product remarkable; it amplifies what the product already causes people to say. Pick the one or two channels where the smallest viable audience already shops and win there before adding more. Ask: who are we competing with on this shelf, and are they Walmart?

**B. Platform algorithms and TikTok virality.**
- He wrote: "Traffic from an algorithm isn’t the point, it’s a random bonus." (S055) and TikTok is "Reporting the weather" rather than creating it (S059).
- Application: treat a viral spike as weather. Judge a launch by repeat purchase, reviews and unprompted mentions among the target group, not by views.

**C. Mega-sale days (9.9, 11.11, 12.12) and vouchers.**
- He wrote that low price is a refuge (S019, S035), that invented cliffs train customers to wait for buzzers (S021), and that a sale is a signal that prompts action (S008).
- Application: participate when the platform requires it, but do not let campaign-day price become the brand promise. Prefer value adds (bundles, limited editions, gifts) over deeper cuts, and check whether repeat buyers now wait for sale days.

**D. KOLs, affiliates and live selling.**
- He wrote that the influencer popularity race is a lottery (S047) and that the way to start traction is to find people with authority and give them an experience that changes them (S003). Buzz alone does not sell (S003).
- Application: choose fewer creators who belong to the target tribe and genuinely use the product, and brief them on what we want a viewer to tell a friend. A live stream is a permission asset if viewers come back; it is a stunt if they only come for the giveaway.

**E. D2C and owned channels (LINE OA, email, community).**
- He wrote: "Permission is anticipated, personal and relevant." (S048) and connection is a form of permission (S004).
- Application: the owned list is the asset. Send only what subscribers would miss; measure unsubscribes and replies, not just open rates.

**F. Brand vs. performance budget.**
- He wrote that direct-response math can sacrifice the brand you seek to build (S005), while admitting ads with consistent budgets can still work (S016).
- Application: ring-fence spend that builds the promise (product, packaging, service, community) from spend judged on ROAS, and do not cut the first to fix the second.

**G. Moonshot portfolio, seat-level challenges (inference, not his words).**
- *The Spiffy Patch (acne patches):* his test for a brand is that "Someone who isn't even using it can tell, from a distance, who made it." (S012). A patch worn on the face is visible in use. Ask: is the patch itself remarkable enough that a classmate asks about it, and what would the wearer say?
- *Linee (oral care):* oral care risks being a mustard category with no otaku (S001). Find the group that cares intensely (for example a specific need or a parent worldview) before broad media.
- *Monchhichi licensed goods:* a character already has a tribe. His line is "Doing it for the tribe, not to them." (S039). Ask what the existing fans want that a generic licensee would not make, and how owning it signals "people like us".
- *Loom by Linee (premium):* race to the top, "A dollar more, and worth it." (S015), and in luxury the price is part of the story (S054). Ask what the extra money buys that a buyer can feel and show; do not discount the premium line to hit volume.
