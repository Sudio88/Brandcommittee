# Stories: Seth Godin

17 stories and case studies he tells in the corpus, his own experiences first, then the cases he uses to teach. Figures are as he tells them. These are illustrations chosen to make a point, not audited case studies; several (Silk, Dutch Boy) are reported without data beyond his summary.

## His own experiences

1. **Spinnaker: the product is the marketing.** As a business-school intern at Spinnaker Software he was given a secret project (illustrated adventure games from Bradbury and Clarke novels). He won engineering time by writing an internal newsletter praising engineers who helped; within months 40 engineers were working on his project without reporting to him. Five titles in a row went gold and saved the company; he did not leave the office for the last 22 days before a RadioShack ship date, and the day after launch Herbie Hancock phoned to say he had stayed up all night playing Fahrenheit 451. Lesson: "that is when I learned marketing is the product." (S003)

2. **Lionel Poilâne and the book that became Purple Cow.** He tried to talk his way into an unbaked sourdough loaf at Poilâne in Paris; Lionel came out, spent hours with him and sent him home with two kilos of starter. After Lionel and his wife died in a helicopter crash, Godin wrote a book so he could dedicate it to him: "The book I wrote, so that I could have a book to dedicate to Lionel, was Purple Cow." (S045). In the 2003 TED talk he uses the bakery as a remarkable product that spread one person at a time (S001). Lesson: generosity plus idiosyncrasy spreads.

3. **Jack Trout's letter.** Before Permission Marketing went to the publisher, Jack Trout wrote that it was based on a flawed idea and should not be published. The book became a bestseller. He tells it as a lesson about uncertainty, not as a grudge: "Everyone has an opinion, but no one has a guarantee." (S020)

4. **The Sauce record label: his "big failure".** He marketed a new high-end audio format to people with very expensive stereos and found the wrong audience: "People with 20,000-dollar stereos don't like new music." (S001). Lesson: figure out who cares and who will raise their hand.

5. **Too early with a music device.** In 2004 he and Dan Lovy launched a device to play music from a hard drive through a stereo. "We were five years too early for early adopters and ten years too early for the beginning of the mass market." (S032). Lesson: being first is insufficient; persistence changes culture.

6. **Your Turn and the 70% re-order.** His book Your Turn was sold mostly in multi-packs to spread the idea; more than 70% of orders came from people who had ordered before (S011). Lesson: build the product so customers have a reason to share it. Grade: single-product anecdote.

7. **Purple Cow in a milk carton.** The first 10,000 copies of Purple Cow shipped in a milk carton (S003). The packaging was part of the remark.

8. **The advertising-supported directory.** One of his first books was an advertising-supported directory paid for by law firms: "This is not something I want to recommend to anybody." (S003). Lesson: choosing your customers chooses your life.

9. **Ten thousand posts, and an AI editor.** He has blogged daily for nearly 10,000 days and writes every word himself; for This Is Strategy he used Claude to ask what he had missed and which claims he had not sustained (S003). Lesson: a queue turns "have to" into "get to".

## Cases he teaches with

10. **Sliced bread.** Otto Rohwedder invented sliced bread, and for about fifteen years no one bought it, until Wonder figured out how to spread the idea (S001). Lesson: the idea's spread, not the patent, decides success.

11. **Silk, Dutch Boy, Aeron, Frank Gehry.** Silk moved into the refrigerated milk case and sales tripled; Dutch Boy's new can sold at a premium because people talked about it; Aeron turned a chair into a status symbol; Gehry changed a city with one building (S001). Lesson: remarkability is designed into the product and placement.

12. **Google's two buttons.** At Yahoo, which had 183 links on its homepage, he watched Google win with two buttons; he sent lost friends to Google because they would not come back for help, which raised his own status (S003). Lesson: the remark is built into the product, and telling must benefit the teller.

13. **PF Flyers.** As a boy he believed the sneakers would make him fast enough to escape a bully; they did not keep the promise (S003). Lesson: launches create tension, and broken promises end trust.

14. **Tesla's door handles and the Cybertruck.** People talked about the Model S's pop-out handles and ludicrous-mode rides; by contrast the Cybertruck was "intentionally divisive" and missed the chance to win the pickup category (S003). Lesson: remarkable and useful beats divisive.

15. **Jaguar's re-logo.** A beloved car brand with few cars on the road had a perfect platform for an electric relaunch; changing the logo threw away awareness assets (S003). Lesson: a logo is not a brand.

16. **The pain reliever in the blue box.** A brand manager spent 100 million dollars trying to interrupt him; he ignored every message and kept buying the yellow box out of habit (S001). Lesson: interruption fails against people without a problem.

17. **This Is Marketing's launch.** The book reached #1 on the Wall Street Journal business list; the collectible edition sold out; he describes a print book as a way to establish "what people like us are talking about" (S037), and later reports more than 250,000 copies in under a year (S044). Lesson: launch to an existing tribe and give them something to talk about together.
