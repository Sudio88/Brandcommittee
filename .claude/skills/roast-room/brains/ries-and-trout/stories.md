# Stories: Al Ries and Jack Trout

20 cases they use, with the lesson as they draw it. Figures are as given in the source. [F] cases come from fetched text (S001, S003). [K] cases are famous from their books but were not in the fetched text, so tell them without quotation marks. All of these are ex-post cases, chosen after the outcome was known (see principles.md, Evidence grade).

1. **Hertz, Avis, National: the ladder.** Hertz got into the mind first and took the top rung; Avis came second and National third. The lesson: acknowledge your rung. [F] (S001) Avis's "We're No. 2, we try harder" campaign is the classic example of a brand using its rung [K].

2. **Honda: cars in New York, motorcycles in Tokyo.** The same company and the same cars, but different first perceptions. "In Japan, Honda got into customers' minds as a manufacturer of motorcycles, and apparently most people don't want to buy a car from a motorcycle company." (S001) The lesson: perception, not product. [F]

3. **A-1 Steak Sauce and A-1 Poultry Sauce.** It seemed logical to stretch a steak sauce brand to chicken. "In the mind, A-1 is not the brand name, but the steak sauce itself." (S001) The lesson: line extension fights the mind. [F]

4. **Federal Express: overnight, then lost.** FedEx owned "overnight", then bought Flying Tiger for $880 million to become a global cargo carrier. "Now Federal Express is a worldwide air cargo company without a worldwide position." (S001) It lost $1.1 billion internationally in 21 months. The lesson: sacrifice, and do not throw away the word. [F]

5. **Coke, Pepsi and Royal Crown.** Pepsi took the opposite of Coke's age. "If old people drink Coke and young people drink Pepsi, there's nobody left to drink Royal Crown cola." (S001) The lesson: duality and the opposite. [F]

6. **Pepsi's teens and Marlboro's cowboy.** The target was narrow, but the market was everyone. The lesson: the target is not the market. [F] (S001)

7. **McDonald's kids and the Burger King opportunity.** McDonald's owns "kids". Burger King could own the older crowd, but only by sacrificing the little kids (S001). The lesson: attributes and the opposite. [F]

8. **IBM copiers and Xerox computers.** "IBM should have dropped copiers and Xerox should have dropped computers years before they finally recognized their mistakes." (S001) The lesson: drop failures early, and a strong name does not travel across categories. [F]

9. **New Coke.** It got more than $1 billion of free publicity and a huge launch, and still failed (S001). The lesson: hype is not a market revolution. [F]

10. **Audi rated ahead of Mercedes and BMW.** German experts said so in the ads; buyers didn't believe it. The lesson: a claim that fights an existing perception is wasted. [F] (S001)

11. **Chevrolet: everything for everyone.** Chevrolet expanded across the range and lost focus. "Sales declined dramatically from 1.5 million cars in 1987 to 0.83 million vehicles in 2001." (S003) The lesson: Law of Expansion. [F]

12. **Subway: one item.** It took the sprawling deli and "radically stripped back the range of products on offer to just one item: the submarine sandwich." (S003) By 2001 it had 12,629 US stores. The lesson: contraction makes a brand. [F]

13. **Holiday Inn Crowne Plaza.** Guests liked the hotel but "they thought it was a bit expensive for a Holiday Inn." (S003) The company later launched its upscale chain under a different name. The lesson: sub-brands confuse, and siblings work. [F]

14. **Budweiser, Miller, Coors: three beers became fourteen.** The extensions added no share and no total consumption (S003). The lesson: Law of Extensions. The authors concede that light beer was a real gap. [F]

15. **Volvo: safety, then sports cars.** For three decades Volvo meant safety. When it pushed into flashy sports cars and convertibles, the brand weakened (S003). The lesson: consistency. [F]

16. **Time Inc. and Fortune.** The business magazine got its own name, not "Time for Business", and Sports Illustrated did likewise (S003). The lesson: Law of Siblings. [F]

17. **Miller Regular's $50 million launch.** A big ad budget behind nothing new produced no publicity and the brand was gone in about a year (S003). The lesson: publicity builds, and ads cannot build a me-too. [F]

18. **Prego "thicker", Kleenex, Xerox: owning the word or the category name.** Prego owned "thick spaghetti sauce" (S003). A "thicker" sauce implies quality and nourishing ingredients (S001). Kleenex and Xerox show a name becoming the category (S003). The lesson: own one word, then let buyers infer the rest. [F]

19. **Kodak and APS; Rinso and Tide.** Kodak invested heavily in a film format during the digital shift. Rinso lost to the new detergent brands (S003). The lesson: don't defend a dying category; launch a new brand for the new one. [F]

20. **7-Up, the Uncola.** It positioned against the cola category rather than against a cola brand, and is the classic Positioning example of relating a new product to an existing ladder [K].
