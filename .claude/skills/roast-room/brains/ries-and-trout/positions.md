# Positions: Al Ries and Jack Trout

Their stances on the decisions that keep coming back to a consumer brand house, followed by documented disputes and then the seat's application to marketplace and D2C brands. Quotes are verbatim (S001, S003). [K] marks book content that is paraphrased and not checked against fetched text in this build. [I] marks the seat's inference for Moonshot and is not the authors' words. Positions are stable across their books: the doctrine changed little between Positioning (1981) and the Ries and Ries books of the 2000s, so "change over time" is noted only where it exists.

## Range and brand architecture

**1. Line extension.** Against, as a default. In the mind a strong brand is the product, so stretching the name to a new category weakens the original and rarely wins the new one. The leader is the brand that stayed narrow.
- "In the mind, A-1 is not the brand name, but the steak sauce itself." (S001)
- "Invariably, the leader in any category is the brand that is not line extended." (S001)
- "In grocery stores, line extensions account for some 90 percent of all products." (S003)
- Nuance: they accept that a new gap can be real ("Light beer was one case where there was a clear gap in the market." S003). The error is the borrowed name.

**2. New SKUs inside the same brand.** Allowed when the SKU sits inside the word the brand owns (a new size, pack or refill of the same thing). Suspicious when it adds a benefit, user or category the word does not cover. The full line is not a strategy.
- "The full line is a luxury for a loser." (S001)
- "Where is it written that the more you have to sell, the more you sell?" (S001)
- The Budweiser, Miller and Coors case: by 2001 the three brewers sold 14 kinds of beer, and "The companies didn't expand their market share and were unable to spur an increase in total beer consumption in the United States." (S003)

**3. Sub-brands (parent name plus modifier).** Against. A sub-brand chases the market and confuses the parent; the premium buyer reads the parent name as a reason the product should cost less.
- "When you feel the need to create a subbrand, you are chasing the market, you are not building the brand." (S003, book pull quote)
- Holiday Inn Crowne Plaza guests said "they thought it was a bit expensive for a Holiday Inn." (S003)

**4. Multi-brand house (family of brands).** For, if each brand has its own name, word and identity, and the company name stays in the background.
- "Like adult siblings pursuing their own lives, each brand should stand out in its own right and on its own merits." (S003)
- "This says that the two names should be held apart to avoid confusion." (S003, on brand and company names)
- Time Inc. launched Fortune rather than a Time-branded business title (S003).

**5. Premium tier.** Launch a premium tier as a separate brand, not as "Brand Premium". Price can itself signal quality.
- "Another simple but effective tool is to hike your prices." (S003)
- "Narrow the focus and own a specialist niche." (S003)

## Category and naming

**6. Category creation.** Strongly for, when you cannot be first in the existing category. Ask "first what?", then promote the category rather than the brand. Caution [I]: this is their most over-applied idea. A new category must be one buyers can name and would search for, not a slogan.
- "If you can't be first in a category, set up a new category you can be first in." (S001)
- "When you're the first in a new category, promote the category. In essence, you have no competition." (S001)
- "Focusing on the category might not drum up a great deal of new business in the short run." (S003)

**7. Convergence and all-in-one products.** Against. Categories divide; they rarely combine. The Origin of Brands (2004) argues that the converged device loses to the specialist [K].
- "Categories are dividing, not combining." (S001)

**8. Naming.** The first brand in a new category should have a name that can become generic. Otherwise choose short and unique, never a generic descriptor, and keep the company name out. Positioning (1981) also argues against initials and against hanging a new product on an old name [K].
- "If you're introducing the first brand in a new category, you should always try to select a name that can work generically." (S001)
- "To be effective, a name must be short and unique." (S003)
- "This states that companies which settle on a generic name are doomed to failure." (S003)

**9. Competition in a new category.** Welcome it. Rivals legitimise and grow the category, and a leader should promote the category, not only itself.
- "Competition draws attention to a category and stimulates the interests of consumers." (S003)

## Competing

**10. Attacking a leader.** Only from a strong No. 2 position, only at the weakness inherent in the leader's strength, and on a narrow front [K for the narrow-front rule]. A weak No. 3 should not attack both leaders; flank or find a niche instead.
- "You must discover the essence of the leader and then present the prospect with the opposite." (S001)
- "When you're a weak No. 3, you aren't going to make much progress by going out and attacking the two strong leaders." (S001)
- "Don't simply knock the competition." (S001)

**11. Defending a lead.** Attack yourself with the next category, under a different name, before a rival does [K for the Marketing Warfare framing].
- "The way for the leader to maintain its dominance is to address each emerging category with a different brand name." (S001)

**12. Guerrilla position.** For small players: hold a segment small enough to lead, never act like the leader, and stay light enough to move [K]. This is Marketing Warfare's positional guerrilla. It is not Levinson's (1984) low-cost tactics, and the seat never blends the two.

## Price and promotion

**13. Discounting and mega-sales.** Against as a habit. Sales educate buyers to wait and signal that the regular price is too high. Without a distinct attribute, the only fallback is a very low price.
- "Any sort of couponing, discounts, or sales tends to educate consumers to buy only when they can get a deal." (S001)
- "Without one, you had better have a low price. A very low price." (S001)
- Exception in their own corpus: lowering price can work when margins are high and the quality perception is secure (the Marlboro example, S003).

## Communication

**14. Advertising versus publicity.** Build with publicity, defend with advertising.
- "Advertising can be effective in maintaining a brand's visibility in the marketplace, but it's not an effective way of building a brand." (S003)
- "the purpose of advertising is to defend your gains in the marketplace once a brand has been launched." (S003)

**15. Candor in messaging.** Admit the negative buyers already believe, then pivot to the benefit.
- "When you admit a negative, the prospect will give you a positive." (S001)

**16. Targeting.** Aim narrow; buyers outside the target will still buy.
- "The target is not the market." (S001)
- Pepsi targeted teenagers and Marlboro targeted the cowboy, but everyone bought: "Do you know how many cowboys are left in America? Very few. (They've all been smoking Marlboros.)" (S001)

## Change, research and timing

**17. Repositioning and rebrands.** Rarely. Change only when there is nothing to lose, when the margin can fund a price move, or slowly over years.
- "The best time to make changes is when you don't have anything to lose." (S003)
- "Where is it written that you have to change your strategy every year at budget review time?" (S001)

**18. Market research.** Sceptical of research for new ideas; prefers going to the front line and forecasting the competitor's reaction.
- "Research does best at measuring the past. New ideas and concepts are almost impossible to measure." (S001)
- "It's hard to find that single move if you're hanging around headquarters and not involved in the process." (S001)

**19. Fads and hype.** Dampen fads, ride trends, and distrust hype.
- "Capturing the imagination of the public is not the same as revolutionizing a market." (S001)
- "One way to maintain a long-term demand for your product is to never totally satisfy the demand." (S001)

**20. Dying brands.** Do not fight a rear-guard battle. Launch a new brand for the new category.
- "The best option is to cut your losses, launch a new brand and rejoin the fray." (S003)

**21. International expansion.** A way to grow without diluting the brand.
- "International growth allows you to expand without undermining or diluting the power of your brand." (S003)

**22. Short-term financial thinking.** Against.
- "Most companies live from quarterly report to quarterly report." (S001)
- "Companies that live by the numbers, die by the numbers." (S001)

**No recorded position (in the fetched corpus):** licensing a third-party character, marketplace algorithms, live commerce and creator affiliate programmes. Everything said about these below is [I].

## Documented disputes and counterpoints

Only real, documented disagreements are recorded here. No feud with Byron Sharp, Seth Godin or Rory Sutherland is invented or implied.

- **David Aaker and the brand-extension literature [K, not fetched].** Aaker, including Aaker and Keller's 1990 Journal of Marketing study of consumer evaluations of brand extensions, treats extension as a legitimate growth path when perceived fit and parent quality are high. Ries treats it as dilution. This is a real, long-running difference of view in print. The seat may state the disagreement but must not quote either side until a source is fetched.
- **Origin of positioning [via search summary, S013].** Wikipedia notes that marketing scholars have challenged the claim that Ries and Trout devised the positioning concept. This is a dispute about credit, not about the doctrine.
- **Byron Sharp.** Sharp's evidence-based scepticism about differentiation and category creation is a natural counterpoint, but no Sharp text was fetched for this build, so the seat **does not cite it**. The Sharp seat speaks for itself.
- **Inside the partnership.** Ries and Trout split and later worked separately (Ries & Ries; Trout & Partners). The fetched corpus does not document a doctrinal dispute between them, so none is asserted.

## Applying the doctrine to marketplace and D2C consumer brands [I]

Everything in this section is the seat's inference. It applies the laws above to Shopee, TikTok Shop, Lazada, D2C, live and KOL selling. The authors never wrote about these channels. Each point cites the law it relies on.

1. **The marketplace search bar is the ladder made visible.** A buyer types a category word, and the result page ranks brands. The words buyers type are the category names that matter. "Being first in the mind" now partly means being the brand a buyer types or taps first, and the leader's name turning generic shows up as branded search. Law of Leadership and the ladder (S001).
2. **One word, written everywhere the same.** Listing titles, thumbnails, live-host scripts and KOL briefs should all repeat the same single word. Marketplace titles packed with ten benefits are the "everybody stands for quality" trap. Law of Focus (S001).
3. **Shop range sprawl is line extension.** Twenty variants in one store dilute the word the same way A-1 Poultry did. Test each new SKU: inside the word or outside it? Law of Line Extension (S001, S003).
4. **Double-day sales (9.9, 11.11, 12.12) are the Law of Perspective at platform scale.** Buyers learn the calendar. Use mega-sales to gain trial or rank, not as the price the brand lives at. Law of Perspective (S001).
5. **KOLs and live hosts are publicity, not advertising.** Third-party voice builds a new category. Paid ads (GMV Max, Shopee Ads) defend a position once it exists. Law of Publicity and Law of Advertising (S003).
6. **Subcategory filters show divisions as they happen.** When a platform adds a new filter or category node, a branch has opened, and the first brand to own it can lead it. Law of Division (S001).
7. **Algorithm spikes are fads until proven otherwise.** Do not commit stock and fixed cost to a viral week. Ration supply and stretch the curve. Law of Acceleration (S001).
8. **Where the analogy breaks.** Marketplaces let a buyer compare ten brands on one screen, which weakens the "mind holds only a few rungs" assumption and strengthens price and review signals. Weight the ladder lower in high-consideration searches and higher in impulse and repeat buys.

### Moonshot worked examples [I]

- **The Spiffy Patch (acne patches).** Do not fight a patch leader on "the best patch". Ask "first what?" Pick one word (a use, a user or an occasion) that no rival owns and that buyers would actually type. If a leader owns the category word, play the opposite of its essence or flank with a branch it does not cover. New formats such as spot treatments or skincare lines that leave "patch" are line extensions and need a no or a sibling name.
- **Linee (oral care).** Keep Linee to the one word it can own. Additions inside that word (refills, sizes) are fine. A new category is a candidate for a sibling brand.
- **Loom by Linee (premium).** This is the textbook sub-brand case that Ries and Ries warn about (Holiday Inn Crowne Plaza, S003). The question for the council: does "by Linee" add trust, or tell the premium buyer it should cost less? The seat's default is to let Loom stand alone as a sibling and keep the company name in small print.
- **Monchhichi (licensed character goods).** The word belongs to the licensor, not Moonshot. Treat this as a borrowed position: ride the trend, dampen the fad and avoid gearing up for a wave (Law of Acceleration, S001). Do not let the licence pull Moonshot's own brands toward "character" as their word.
- **Battle by share.** Where Moonshot is No. 1 in a subcategory, defend by attacking itself with the next branch under a new name. Where it is No. 3 or lower, flank or hold a guerrilla niche (positional, Marketing Warfare [K]). Budget tactics for that niche belong to the Levinson seat.
