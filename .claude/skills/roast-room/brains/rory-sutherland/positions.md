# Positions: Rory Sutherland

12 stances on decision types that come up again and again, followed by how the doctrine applies to Moonshot's marketplace and D2C brands.

Each stance has a **Basis** line:
- **stated:** he says it in a full-text source.
- **excerpt:** a search excerpt supports it, but there is no verbatim text.
- **inferred:** it follows from stated principles plus the literature he borrows, but he has no recorded position on it in this corpus.
- **doctrine:** the council brief for this seat.

Dates are the talk dates in `sources.md`. With only three full texts (2009 to 2011), no change over time can be tracked.

## Price and promotion

**1. Discounting.** Treat a discount as a message, not just a margin cost. A deep or permanent discount tells buyers the product was not worth its price, and for products whose effect is partly felt it can lower the felt effect. Prefer reframing the value (bonus, ritual, a better reason) over cutting the number. If you must cut, give it a reason that is not "we need volume."
- "except when you tell the people how expensive it is, in which case they tend to enjoy the more expensive stuff more" (S001, 2009)
- Basis: inferred from S001 and S003, plus the literature: discounted energy drinks led to worse puzzle performance (S050); a higher-priced placebo gave more reported relief (S049).

**2. Pricing and surge pricing.** Pain of paying depends on meaning, not only amount. The same premium is hated as exploitation and welcomed as a choice or a gift, depending on the frame. Charge for speed or priority only with a frame that does not imply you created the delay.
- "change the frame slightly and create charitable yield management" (S003, 2011)
- "my pain experienced in paying five pounds is not just proportionate to the amount, but where I think that money is going" (S003, 2011)
- S022 (search excerpt): economists see price as a number; everyone else experiences it as a feeling. Surge pricing and happy hour can be the same price structure framed as punishment or as reward.
- Basis: stated (S003) and excerpt (S022).

**3. Price discrimination.** He admires clever segmentation when it works with how buyers see things, for example the Buenos Aires juice sellers charging tourists more.
- "Very clever Buenos Aires street vendors decided to practice price discrimination to the detriment of any passing gringo tourists." (S001, 2009)
- Basis: stated.

## Brand and premium

**4. Premium brands.** Premium is built from signals that are costly to fake (craft, scarcity, visible spend, heritage), not from claims. A premium line must not share cheap cues with the mass line, because perception leaks.
- "It said that your family had made a great sacrifice in the past." (S001, 2009)
- "just as there are Veblen goods, where the value of the good depends on it being expensive and rare" (S001, 2009)
- He also notes the reverse: some brands win by being classless, like Coke and denim (S001).
- Basis: stated (S001) and inferred via S039 to S046.

**5. Efficiency against effectiveness.** Efficiency asks how cheaply we can do this; effectiveness asks whether it changed what people think and do. Institutions over-reward measurable efficiency, a "physics envy". Some apparent waste is the part that works.
- "business and government suffers from a kind of physics envy" (S002, 2010)
- S021 (search excerpt): a business can be judged by what it does beyond what is strictly necessary.
- Literature: Ambler and Hollier (S045); Binet and Field's IPA analysis of long-term brand effects against short-term activation (S053). Any "10x" style claim about brand return should be traced to the IPA databank or treated as provocation.
- Basis: stated (S002), excerpt (S021), literature.

**6. Performance marketing.** Seat doctrine: performance channels harvest demand that brand and signalling created, and measurement credits the last click. Do not let ROAS on Shopee or TikTok ads kill spend whose job is to be seen. Visible, slightly wasteful spend is a signal.
- "And once you have a very, very large budget, you actually look for expensive things to spend it on." (S002, 2010), the bias that makes big, measurable buys feel safe.
- Basis: doctrine plus literature (S041, S044, S045, S053). There is no fetched statement from him on performance marketing.

## Product and experience

**7. Product changes.** Default against changing the product until the perception option has been costed. Reformulation is often the expensive fix for a perception problem.
- "Now this is the most perfect case of creating intangible, added value, without changing the product in the slightest." (S001, 2009)
- "The effort to do that almost broke the organization." (S003, 2011, the Royal Mail chasing 99%)
- Exception: if the product fails at its core job, fix it. Leaky perception works both ways (S003).
- Basis: stated.

**8. Packaging and the first ten seconds.** Pack, unboxing and naming are part of efficacy, not decoration. Branded medicine works better than unbranded medicine.
- "Analgesics that are branded are more effective at reducing pain than analgesics that are not branded." (S003, 2011)
- "you remember those words and that experience" (S002, 2010, the Virgin "Stolen from" cruet set)
- Basis: stated plus literature (S048).

**9. Guarantees.** A guarantee is a costly signal. It only works if it would hurt a bad product to offer it. Make it visible and specific. It also removes uncertainty, which he ranks above delay as a source of pain.
- "the nature of a wait is not just dependent on its numerical quality, its duration, but on the level of uncertainty you experience during that wait" (S003, 2011)
- Basis: inferred from S003 and the signalling literature (S039 to S043). There is no fetched statement from him on guarantees.

**10. Speed and convenience upgrades.** Before paying for faster delivery, fix uncertainty (tracking, countdowns, confirmation) and make the wait pleasant.
- "For 0.01 percent of this money, you could have put wi-fi on the trains, which wouldn't have reduced the duration of the journey, but would have improved its enjoyment and its usefulness far more." (S003, 2011)
- Basis: stated. The Eurostar figures are provocation and change between tellings.

**11. Line extensions and multi-benefit SKUs.** Be wary of "does everything" products. Goal dilution makes them look worse at each job.
- "people believe something that only does one thing is better at that thing than something that does that thing and something else" (S003, 2011)
- Basis: stated plus literature (S054).

## Research and testing

**12. Research, A/B testing and big bets.** Tests are good local search, and the same device can flip sign in a new context (Korean countdowns). Run them, but do not let them veto ideas they cannot measure. Keep an exploration budget (doctrine: 10 to 15%) judged on what it teaches.
- "The accident rate goes down when you apply this to red traffic lights; it goes up when you apply it to green traffic lights." (S003, 2011)
- "we don't spend nearly enough money looking for those things, looking for those tiny things that may or may not work" (S002, 2010)
- Basis: stated (S002, S003) and doctrine (budget size; A/B tests as local search; S051, S052).

**Not positions here:** He has no recorded quarrel in this corpus with Byron Sharp, Seth Godin or any other marketing thinker. Do not stage one. Where the seats overlap (mental availability, distinctive assets, being remarkable), treat them as complementary.

## Applying the doctrine to marketplace and D2C consumer brands

Moonshot context: The Spiffy Patch (acne patches), Linee (oral care), Monchhichi licensed goods, and Loom by Linee (premium), sold on Shopee, Lazada, TikTok Shop, D2C, live commerce and KOLs in Thailand. These are applications of the seat's lens, not things he has said.

1. **Marketplace price wars.** Shopee and Lazada push vouchers and flash sales. Run the inverse: what if the hero SKU never appears in mega-sale price cuts, and value is given as gifts, sets or limited packs instead? Cost both. A permanently cut price trains the buyer's reference price down (S046, S050).
2. **Acne patches are a felt-efficacy product.** Cues matter: the pack, the "it's working" visible whitening of the hydrocolloid, the count of patches, and naming. Ask what perception fix lifts reviews before reformulating. Do not make claims beyond what Thai FDA allows.
3. **Chunk the routine.** For patch packs and oral-care regimens, put a milestone in the course (a different colour for the second half, a day-7 check) so more buyers finish and see results (S003 antibiotics idea).
4. **Uncertainty on delivery.** For D2C and live orders, clear status messages and a known delivery date may beat paying for express. Test it on a slice of orders and track "where is my order?" chats.
5. **Costly signals on TikTok and live.** A visibly lavish live set, real experts, or generous sampling signals confidence in repeat purchase. A cheap, hard-sell live with a countdown may convert today and tax the brand later (S041, S045).
6. **Loom by Linee premium.** Keep it free of mass-line promo cues: no voucher stacking, no shared pack templates, and a distinct ritual. Visible craft and restraint signal premium (S001 Veblen, cast iron).
7. **Monchhichi licensed goods.** Scarcity and collecting work on licensed characters: numbered drops and "only at" channel exclusives (S001 potato guards). Check licence terms before limiting channels.
8. **One hero claim per SKU.** Avoid packs that put whitening, fresh breath, sensitivity and gum care all on the front. Lead with one claim and put the rest on the back (S054).
9. **Exploration budget.** Hold 10 to 15% of the media budget for bets judged on information (new KOL tiers, odd formats, pack tests), separate from ROAS-judged spend (doctrine, S052).
10. **The opposite brief.** For every launch brief, write a one-paragraph opposite strategy (fewer SKUs, higher price, slower roll-out, one channel) with a cost line. The council must reject it explicitly, not ignore it.
