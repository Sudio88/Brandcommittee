# Frameworks: Rory Sutherland

15 frameworks, grouped by the job they do: reframe, signal, design the experience, allocate and test. Each one has an evidence grade.

- **A:** the effect rests on peer-reviewed literature he borrows (cited by S id).
- **B:** documented in his own talks with a plausible mechanism, but no traced effect size.
- **C:** provocation or seat doctrine. Use it to generate options, never to forecast.

Quotes are verbatim from the three fetched TED transcripts (S001 to S003). The Alchemy book text was not available (see `books.md`).

---

## Reframe

### 1. Perception-first costing (grade B)
- **What it is:** Before paying to change the product, price the cheapest change to how it is perceived that gets the same gain.
- **Parts:** (1) Measure what buyers believe today against what is true. (2) If belief is worse than reality, fix the belief. (3) Price both fixes and compare the gain per baht.
- **When to use:** Any proposal to reformulate, re-tool, speed up, or "improve quality" in response to complaints.
- **How to score:** Compute the gap between perceived and actual performance. A large gap means a perception fix comes first. Score each option on gain per baht, not on gain alone.
- **Quote:** "Now, if your perception is much worse than your reality, what on earth are you doing trying to change the reality?" (S003)
- **Story:** The Royal Mail spent heavily to move next-day delivery from 98% to 99%, while the public guessed it was around 50 to 60% (S003).
- **Sources:** S001, S003.

### 2. Hedonic opportunity cost (grade C as numbers, B as method)
- **What it is:** For any large spend, ask what the same money would buy in felt experience if spent on perception.
- **Parts:** State the reality spend. List perception spends that improve the same experience (wi-fi, comfort, theatre). Compare.
- **When to use:** Capex, reformulation, and speed or logistics upgrades.
- **How to score:** Two columns: the reality fix and the best perception fix, each with cost and expected experience gain. The Eurostar figures are provocation and never benchmarks.
- **Quote:** "Now what is the hedonic opportunity cost on spending six billion pounds on those railway tracks?" (S001)
- **Sources:** S001, S002, S003, S024 (search excerpt: software changes could cut rail journey times at a fraction of HS2's cost).

### 3. Inverse strategy, the opposite of a good idea (grade C)
- **What it is:** Generate the opposite of the proposed plan, cost it with the same rigour, and keep whichever wins.
- **Parts:** Flip one axis at a time: price (down to up), speed (faster to slower but nicer), breadth (more to fewer features), availability (everywhere to scarce), spend (hidden to visible).
- **When to use:** Every time the room converges quickly. Convergence means only one direction was searched.
- **How to score:** The inverse must be costed, not just named. If it beats the original on cost per unit of perceived value, it wins.
- **Quote:** "Well, the numbers all seem to add up, but before I present this idea, I'll show it to some really crazy people to see if they can come up with something better." (S003, said as what logical planners never say)
- **Sources:** S003; the rule appears in the Alchemy rules listed in S035 and S036 (search excerpt), and in podcast titles S030 to S032.

### 4. Name it again: rebranding the thing itself (grade B)
- **What it is:** Change what a thing is called or how it is framed, and the reaction to it changes.
- **Parts:** The same activity with a different label ("a year off"), a different frame of comparison (better than the Germans), or a different destination for the money (charity yield management).
- **When to use:** Price rises, shortages, waits, unpopular changes, and product limitations.
- **How to score:** Does the new frame change the meaning without changing the facts? Is it honest? Would a buyer who later learns the facts feel cheated? If yes, reject it.
- **Quote:** "What you call them actually affects how you react to them, viscerally and morally." (S003)
- **Sources:** S001 (Frederick the Great, Diamond Shreddies), S003.

### 5. Choose the frame of reference (grade B)
- **What it is:** Value is judged against a comparison, so choose the comparison.
- **Parts:** The absolute number (98%), the comparison (better than a rival), and the meaning (where the money goes).
- **When to use:** Claims, review summaries, and price communication.
- **How to score:** List three possible reference points and pick the one the buyer already cares about.
- **Quote:** "Choose your frame of reference and the perceived value, and therefore, the actual value is completely transformed." (S003)
- **Sources:** S003, S022 (search excerpt: to economists price is a number; to everyone else it is a feeling).

## Signal

### 6. Costly signalling: expense is the message (grade A for the theory, B for his use)
- **What it is:** A signal is believable when it would be too expensive for a weaker sender to fake. Visible spend on ads, packaging, sampling or guarantees tells the buyer the firm expects to be around and expects repeat purchase.
- **Parts:** Zahavi's handicap (S039) and Grafen's formal proof (S040). Nelson: for experience goods, the amount of advertising is itself the information (S041). Kihlstrom and Riordan (S042) and Milgrom and Roberts (S043): ad spend and price as quality signals. Ambler and Hollier: the "waste" is the part that works (S045). Sahni and Nair ran a field experiment in mobile search: ads raised consumer response in a way consistent with signalling, with larger effects for less-known restaurants (S044; check the paper for exact sizes).
- **When to use:** New launches, new channels, premium lines, and any time a cheaper execution is proposed to "save money."
- **How to score:** Would a fly-by-night competitor be able to copy this spend? If yes, it signals nothing. Is the cost visible to the buyer? If not, it signals nothing.
- **Quote:** "It said that your family had made a great sacrifice in the past." (S001, cast-iron jewellery that outranked gold for 50 years)
- **Sources:** S001, S009 (search excerpt: he names costly signalling theory as one explanation for invention hype), S010 (search excerpt: spending as positional display), S039 to S045.

### 7. Scarcity and forbidden fruit (grade B)
- **What it is:** Restricting something can make it more wanted than promoting it.
- **Parts:** A visible barrier, a hint of privilege, and a way for people to get it anyway.
- **When to use:** Launches, limited editions, and licensed character drops.
- **How to score:** Is the scarcity real or at least honest? Does it raise the status of owning it?
- **Quote:** "if something is worth guarding, it's worth stealing" (S001)
- **Sources:** S001.

### 8. Price as a quality cue (grade A)
- **What it is:** Price changes the experience itself, not just the purchase decision.
- **Parts:** Plassmann et al.: the same wine labelled at a higher price was rated more pleasant and lit up reward-linked brain areas more (S046). Goldstein et al.: in blind tastings, non-experts did not prefer pricier wine (S047). Waber et al.: a placebo at a higher price gave more reported pain relief (S049). Shiv, Carmon and Ariely: discounted energy drinks led to worse puzzle performance (S050).
- **When to use:** Every discount, every cheaper SKU, and every premium launch.
- **How to score:** Does the move lower the price the buyer remembers? For efficacy products (patches, oral care) treat that as a risk to perceived efficacy.
- **Quote:** "there is no correlation between quality and enjoyment in wine, except when you tell the people how expensive it is, in which case they tend to enjoy the more expensive stuff more" (S001)
- **Sources:** S001, S003, S046, S047, S049, S050.

### 9. Leaky perception and branded efficacy (grade A)
- **What it is:** People cannot fully separate the product from its cues, so pack, brand and setting change how well it seems to work.
- **Parts:** Branthwaite and Cooper: branded analgesics outperformed unbranded ones, and branded placebo outperformed unbranded placebo, for headache relief (S048).
- **When to use:** Packaging, unboxing, and claims for products whose results are partly felt (acne, freshness, whitening).
- **How to score:** List every cue in the first 10 seconds of use. Remove any that says cheap, generic or unsafe.
- **Quote:** "Analgesics that are branded are more effective at reducing pain than analgesics that are not branded." (S003)
- **Sources:** S003, S048, S049.

### 10. Goal dilution: one thing done well (grade A)
- **What it is:** A product that does one thing is believed to do it better than a product that does several.
- **Parts:** Zhang, Fishbach and Kruglanski's dilution model (S054). His examples are Google against portals, and combined TV and DVD players.
- **When to use:** Line extensions, two-in-one SKUs, and "all-in-one" claims.
- **How to score:** Count the claims on the front of the pack. More than one hero claim means a dilution risk.
- **Quote:** "people believe something that only does one thing is better at that thing than something that does that thing and something else" (S003)
- **Sources:** S003, S054.

## Design the experience

### 11. Uncertainty beats delay (grade B)
- **What it is:** A known wait feels shorter than an unknown wait. Remove uncertainty before removing time.
- **Parts:** Countdown, progress, and confirmation.
- **When to use:** Delivery times, live-stream queues, pre-orders, and customer service.
- **How to score:** Does the buyer know what happens next and when? If not, fix that before paying for faster logistics.
- **Quote:** "the nature of a wait is not just dependent on its numerical quality, its duration, but on the level of uncertainty you experience during that wait" (S003)
- **Caution:** The Korean countdown worked on red lights and raised accidents on green lights (S003). The same device can backfire in a new context, so test it.
- **Sources:** S003.

### 12. Control and chunking (grade B; grade A for control via S055)
- **What it is:** The same experience is better tolerated when people feel in control, and long tasks are finished more often with a milestone in the middle.
- **Parts:** Seligman and Maier on control and helplessness (S055); chunking courses into stages (S003).
- **When to use:** Regimens (patch courses, oral-care routines), subscriptions, and loyalty.
- **How to score:** Does the routine have a visible midpoint? Can the buyer choose, pause or swap?
- **Quote:** "The likelihood that people will get to the end is much greater when there is a milestone somewhere in the middle." (S003)
- **Sources:** S003, S055.

### 13. Interface and defaults (grade B)
- **What it is:** Behaviour follows the interface, so change the default and the choice changes.
- **Parts:** What appears first, what is opt-in, and what is hidden.
- **When to use:** Checkout, bundles, subscription prompts, and landing pages.
- **How to score:** Name the default. Ask whether the buyer would choose it if it were not the default.
- **Quote:** "The reason is that the interface fundamentally determines the behavior." (S001)
- **Caution:** His online-banking forecast of "twice as many" users (S002) is a prediction, not a result.
- **Sources:** S001, S002.

## Allocate and test

### 14. The four-quadrant spend map and the Chief Detail Officer (grade C)
- **What it is:** Plot initiatives by cost (high or low) against effect (high or low): strategy, consultancy, trivia, and the unnamed quadrant of cheap things that might have huge effects. Someone must own that last quadrant, with power but no budget.
- **When to use:** Quarterly budgeting, and whenever the room only discusses large line items.
- **How to score:** Count the low-cost bets in the plan. Zero means the plan only searches where big budgets go.
- **Quote:** "What is completely lacking is a class of people who have immense amounts of power, but no money at all." (S002)
- **Sources:** S002, S021 (search excerpt: a business is judged by what it does beyond what is strictly necessary; peripheral Kano-type features shape ratings).

### 15. Exploration budget and A/B tests as local search (grade C as doctrine, A for the exploration logic)
- **What it is:** Seat doctrine: ring-fence 10 to 15% of spend for exploration and judge it on information value, not on ROAS. A/B tests optimise near where you already are; they cannot find a different hill.
- **Parts:** March's exploration against exploitation trade-off (S052). Binet and Field's IPA databank work separates short-term activation from long-term brand effects (S053), which is where claims that brand building pays more over time trace.
- **When to use:** Budget splits across Shopee, Lazada and TikTok ads, and any time a test result is used to kill a bold idea.
- **How to score:** For each experiment, write down what you will learn if it fails. No answer means it is not exploration.
- **Quote:** "but which, if they do work, can have a success absolutely out of proportion to their expense, their efforts and the disruption they cause" (S002)
- **Sources:** S002, S052, S053. The percentage is seat doctrine; no fetched source gives it.
