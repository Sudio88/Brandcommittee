# Stories: Rory Sutherland

16 anecdotes he tells in the fetched talks, with an evidence label for each:
- **documented case:** a real campaign or event with a traceable outcome.
- **anecdote:** a real story whose outcome is untraced.
- **provocation:** invented or exaggerated to make a point.
- **study:** borrowed from research, with the source traced.

Figures are given as he tells them.

1. **The Eurostar and the supermodels (provocation).** Engineers spent about six billion pounds to cut about 40 minutes off the London to Paris journey. For a fraction of that money you could hire the world's top supermodels to serve free Chateau Petrus, and "people will ask for the trains to be slowed down" (S001). Later tellings add wi-fi for 0.01% of the cost (S003). The figures change between tellings: three billion pounds "in change" (S001), five billion (S002), then "six million pounds" spent and five million in change (S003). **Provocation, not a case study.** Use it only to ask about hedonic opportunity cost. (S001, S002, S003, S004, S005, S006, S024)

2. **Frederick the Great's royal potatoes (anecdote, historically disputed).** Peasants refused potatoes even when they were compulsory. The king declared them a royal vegetable and had the field guarded loosely, and theft spread the crop. "What he'd effectively done is he'd re-branded the potato." (S001) He notes the story is also attributed to other kings. Lesson: scarcity and status beat compulsion. (S001)

3. **Diamond Shreddies (documented campaign).** An Ogilvy Canada intern, Hunter Somerville, relaunched a square cereal as "Diamond Shreddies" by turning it 45 degrees, with spoof research and a "combo pack." Lesson: intangible value with zero product change. Sales outcome not given in the corpus. (S001)

4. **Cast-iron jewellery, 1813 (anecdote with a historical basis).** Wealthy Prussians gave their gold to the war effort and wore iron replicas, which became the highest-status jewellery for 50 years. Lesson: a costly sacrifice, made visible, is a signal. (S001)

5. **Ataturk and the veil (unverified anecdote).** He tells the story that Ataturk made prostitutes wear the veil to discourage it, and adds, "I can't verify that fully, but it does not matter." (S001) Lesson: reframe through association rather than ban. **Do not cite it as fact.**

6. **Smiley-face speed signs (anecdote, figures untraced).** Signs that flash a smile or a frown cost "about 10 percent" of a speed camera and "prevent twice as many accidents" (S001). The figures are untraced in the corpus; treat them as provocation. Lesson: persuasion over compulsion.

7. **Italian penalty points (anecdote).** Licences start with 12 points that are taken away, because loss aversion motivates more than gaining points. (S001)

8. **Wine and price (study).** "there is no correlation between quality and enjoyment in wine, except when you tell the people how expensive it is" (S001). He credits wine economists. This traces to Goldstein et al. (S047) and to Plassmann et al.'s fMRI study (S046).

9. **The Virgin salt and pepper set (documented detail).** The Upper Class cruet set is engraved "Stolen from Virgin Atlantic Airways upper-class," so people remember it years after they forget the aircraft type. Lesson: small, witty details carry the brand. (S002)

10. **The Lydmar Hotel lift (anecdote).** The lift buttons choose the music genre instead of the floor, at an estimated 500 to 1,000 pounds, and are more memorable than a 500,000 dollar room refit. (S002)

11. **Lentils for vaccination (borrowed from Esther Duflo; study-based).** A kilo of lentils plus a social event raised child immunisation. Lesson: tiny incentives can beat big programmes. Trace it to Duflo and colleagues' field work before quoting effect sizes. (S002)

12. **"Tell me my balance" (proposal, forecast untested).** He advised a bank to make the balance opt-in rather than the default on login, and predicted twice as many users logging on three times as often, for about 50 pounds. **A forecast, not a result.** (S002)

13. **Heathrow Terminal 5 signage (anecdote).** A magnificent building undone by contradictory signs. He sent five suggestions, which he says were acted on. Lesson: "Terminal 5 syndrome," where big things get attention and the small user interface fails. (S002)

14. **London Underground countdown boards (anecdote, widely cited).** The best gain in passenger satisfaction per pound came from dot-matrix arrival boards, not more trains. A known seven-minute wait beats an unknown four-minute one. (S003)

15. **Korean traffic-light countdowns (anecdote with a test).** Countdowns on red lights cut accidents; on green lights they raised accidents, because drivers sped up. Lesson: a lever can flip sign in a new context. (S003)

16. **Royal Mail 98% to 99% (anecdote).** Lifting next-day delivery from 98% to 99% "almost broke the organization," while the public guessed 50 to 60%. Tell people the 98% first, or that it beats Germany. (S003)

Also told: the e-cigarette and the drinks party ("a philosopher" with a cigarette, "friendless" without one) and pensioners against the young unemployed, where control and choice explain the difference in happiness (S003); charity yield management at toll bridges (S003); and the branded analgesics finding, traced to Branthwaite and Cooper (S048) (S003).
