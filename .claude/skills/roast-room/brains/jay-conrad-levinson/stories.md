# Stories: Jay Conrad Levinson

10 stories, cases and reference events this seat reuses. The retrieved corpus holds few long anecdotes from Levinson himself; most "stories" are his career, his dated follow-up routine, and examples from his books and the house blog. Figures are as the sources give them and are not independently verified.

1. **From the Marlboro Man to the small business.** He was a creative director at Leo Burnett in London and a senior vice-president at J. Walter Thompson, and is credited with work on big-brand campaigns such as the Marlboro Man and the Jolly Green Giant, before writing for owners with no budget (S009, S016, S017). Campaign calls the irony out: the big-brand insider became the champion of the small advertiser, and "Levinson's book, Guerrilla Marketing: Secrets For Making Big Profits From Your Small Business, was an industry game-changer." (S009) His exact role on each brand is described loosely in the sources; treat it as attribution, not fact. Lesson: big-budget methods do not transfer to small firms; small firms need a different arsenal.

2. **The 1984 book and the brand that followed.** Guerrilla Marketing (1984) became a series of dozens of titles and a training brand; sales claims range from 21 million to 25 million to "50M+" depending on the seller (S014, S025, S032). Lesson for the seat: sales of a book are not evidence the tactics work; this is survivorship, so the corpus is graded conceptual.

3. **The follow-up calendar.** His model guerrilla sends a thank-you within 48 hours, contacts again at 30 days, previews a sale at six months, and asks for three referral names at nine months (S002). Lesson: the subsequent sale is planned, dated and owned, not hoped for.

4. **The 68 percent leak.** He repeats that most lost business is lost to apathy after the sale: "a numbing 68% of all business lost in America is lost due to apathy after the sale" (S002), "Nearly 70 percent" in a trade article (S006). Lesson: the leak after the sale is bigger than the leak before it. Caution: no study is cited and the number drifts.

5. **Power partners and brochure racks.** In the fusion excerpt, partners such as a printer and a designer, or an estate-planning attorney and an insurer, share customers, swap content and links, and set "brochure racks on the front counters" of each other's businesses (S007). Lesson: reach that someone else already owns can be borrowed for nearly nothing.

6. **Fox Sports, NASCAR and iRacing.** The house blog's pandemic example of fusion: "Fox Sports, NASCAR and iRacing came together in a Guerrilla Fusion Marketing extravaganza." (S025) Lesson: when the main channel closes, a partner's channel can carry the brand. Note this is the organization's example, post-Levinson.

7. **Twenty keys out of a hundred and twenty.** For a spa trade magazine he picked 20 tactics from "a full 120 described in my book" and told owners to try them all (S006). Lesson: the arsenal is a menu to test, not a list to buy.

8. **Nine penetrations, 27 exposures.** The 4th edition's rule that a message must reach a prospect's mind about nine times, which takes about 27 exposures, before purchase (S001). Lesson: patience and consistency are arithmetic, not virtue. Caution: rhetorical rule of thumb; the later "3-7-27" rule is not his.

9. **Boston, 2007: the stunt that became an emergency.** A guerrilla campaign for Cartoon Network's Aqua Teen Hunger Force film placed battery-powered LED placards around Boston and nearby cities; on 31 January 2007 authorities treated them as possible bombs, the response shut down parts of the city, Turner and its agency Interference agreed to pay $2 million, and general manager Jim Samples resigned (S018, S019, S020, S021). Not Levinson's campaign. Lesson for this seat: amazement without clear attribution and a public-safety read is a liability, and stunts come from the exploration budget, never the continuity budget.

10. **The teacher at the end.** He kept teaching to the end; On Purpose Magazine notes "Jay passed away October 10th of 2013 after battling cancer" (S014), and obituaries called him the father of guerrilla marketing (S015, S016). Lesson: the doctrine he practised on himself was the long, consistent process.
