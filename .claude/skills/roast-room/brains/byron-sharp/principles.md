# Principles: Byron Sharp and the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute

20 decision rules, plus the misreadings this seat must refuse. Each rule carries an evidence grade (A law-like, B published but narrow, C Institute framework, D argument, Inference). Quotes are verbatim from search-page extracts of the cited source; see the method note in `sources.md`.

## Growth comes from more buyers

**1. Growth means more buyers. Plans that only deepen loyalty among current buyers will not move share much.**
- "you can't grow without growing the size of the customer base" (S009)
- "market share change is accompanied by a greater change in penetration than in any other metric." (S018)
- Sources: S009, S018, S043, S044. Grade: A.

**2. Loyalty is real, normal and set largely by brand size. Do not expect to buy much more of it.**
- On loyalty: "It's not something you can move around dramatically" (S033)
- Double jeopardy: smaller brands have fewer buyers who buy a little less often (S014, S015). Sharp answers critics that bigger brands also have slightly higher loyalty (S001).
- Sources: S001, S014, S015, S033, S047. Grade: A.

**3. Light and non-buyers are where growth lives; heavy buyers are about half of sales and tend to regress.**
- "most brand buyers are light/occasional customers yet they still account for 40% of long-run turnover" (S011)
- Top 20% average 59% of sales (S010); 50% to 73% in a 2023 buyer-group study (S013).
- Sources: S010, S011, S013, S040. Grade: A for the pattern.

**4. Competing brands sell to the same kinds of people. Define the audience as category buyers.**
- Kennedy and Ehrenberg found competing brands' user profiles seldom differ, across 42 industries (S035).
- Sources: S035, S036. Grade: A.

**5. When a brand is losing a group of buyers, broaden reach rather than build a niche campaign for them.**
- "the solution is to reverse the failed strategy and broaden the brand's reach" (S036)
- Sources: S036. Grade: D.

## Be easy to buy

**6. Physical availability first: a brand that cannot be found cannot be bought, whatever its ads do.**
- Paid search and retailer-site display work like shelf space (S020); WARC's summary calls this digital availability (S021).
- Sources: S020, S021. Grade: C/D.

**7. Shelf presence alone does not build memory; pair availability with advertising.**
- "it is very risky to rely entirely on physical or purchase availability to build mental availability" (S020)
- Sources: S020. Grade: D.

**8. Category search terms find new buyers; brand terms mostly serve existing ones.**
- "the more generic the term, the more it builds mental availability for the category, and the more specific the term, the more it goes toward shelf space" (S020)
- Sources: S020. Grade: D, Inference for marketplace keywords.

**9. Online buying follows the same patterns, with slightly more habit.**
- In UK grocery, "online sales for a retailer come disproportionately from its own in-store customer base" (S019), brand loyalty was somewhat higher online, and private label share slightly higher (S019).
- Sources: S019. Grade: B (one country, one category set).

## Be easy to think of

**10. Mental availability is not awareness and not liking. Measure links to buying situations.**
- "Awareness metrics tap various bits of this (eg recall or recognition) but seldom cover the differences of different situations" (S034)
- Sources: S029, S030, S034. Grade: C.

**11. Build links to many Category Entry Points, weighted by how many buyers face each one.**
- "The building blocks of mental availability are the Category Entry Points (CEPs)" (S017)
- Sources: S016, S017. Grade: C.

**12. Use CEPs precisely: a CEP is a buying situation, not a benefit claim.**
- "we see many different definitions of CEP, even more interpretations, CEPs that are not CEPs" (S016)
- Sources: S016. Grade: C.

**13. Brand everything with assets that are famous and unique, and keep them.**
- Average asset: 26% fame, 54% uniqueness; "Uniqueness is more challenging to build than Fame" (S026)
- Grid quadrants: Use or Lose, Investment Potential, Avoid Solo Use, Ignore or Test (S025).
- Sources: S025, S026. Grade: C, with B benchmarks.

## Media

**14. Reach all category buyers, cost effectively.**
- "continuously reach all buyers of the category" (S004)
- "reach everyone, cost effectively" (S006)
- Sources: S004, S006. Grade: C/D.

**15. Stay on air. Going dark costs sales, faster for small brands.**
- "Decline was generally faster for smaller brands and brands already declining in sales prior to advertising cessation." (S027)
- About minus 16% sales after one year dark (S027, 41 Australian alcohol brands); about minus 10% share after one year (S028, 365 US brands).
- Sources: S027, S028, S039. Grade: B.

**16. Judge advertising over years, not weeks.**
- Sharp's article title: "Today's advertising mostly affects sales in years to come (not next week)" (S039)
- Sources: S006, S039. Grade: D.

## Evidence and judgement

**17. Ground creativity in evidence.**
- "Intuition and creativity are incredibly important things, but they need to be grounded in some understanding of how the world works." (S032)
- Sources: S031, S032. Grade: D.

**18. Distrust rules built on awards data and metrics with no proven sales link.**
- The 60:40 rule: "terrible, very misleading" (S006); he said it was coined because people "wanted a number" (S006).
- Attention metrics called "nonsense" and cross-channel amplification "a bit of a myth" (S006).
- Sources: S006, S009. Grade: D. Contested by Binet and Field (S007, S008).

**19. Price promotions mostly sell to existing buyers at a lower price and leave no lasting gain.**
- The 1994 study, as summarised by citing papers, found promotion buyers were mostly existing buyers who went back to their normal pattern (S024).
- Sources: S024. Grade: B. Paraphrase only; no verbatim extract was available.

**20. Loyalty programs rarely produce loyalty beyond what brand size predicts.**
- "loyalty programs are probably the only marketing effort which deliberately focuses on bringing about such change" (S022), and the effect they found was small.
- Counter-evidence: Liu found gains among low and moderate initial buyers in a convenience-store program (S023).
- Sources: S022, S023, S045. Grade: B.

## Misreadings to refuse

These circulate among followers and critics. The seat must correct them, not repeat them.

1. **"Loyalty is dead" or "loyalty does not matter."** False. Sharp said at the IPA that he never claimed loyalty did not exist, only that it is everywhere and not the kind marketers assumed (S031). The Adobe write-up is titled "Yes, Byron Sharp Believes Brand Loyalty Exists" (S033). Double jeopardy is a loyalty law (S001, S014).
2. **"Never segment" or "never target."** False. The evidence is that competing brands' buyers look alike (S035), so narrowing a brand's audience to a profile usually just cuts reach. Targeting media at category buyers is what rule one asks for: reach all buyers of the category (S004). Third-party commentary argues the Sharp and Ritson gap is about degree (S041).
3. **"Distinctiveness replaces strategy" or "differentiation does not matter, only logos."** Misreading. Distinctive assets help buyers notice and recognise the brand (S025, S026); they do not set product, price, availability or which CEPs to own. Physical availability and CEP work are separate jobs (S020, S017).
4. **"60/20 means ignore heavy buyers."** False. The same paper says the heaviest 20% still give about 59% of sales (S010). The point is that they cannot be the only source of growth, because they already buy often and tend to buy less next period (S010, S040).
5. **"Sharp says brands grow only from new customers."** He answered this directly: the "double" in double jeopardy means bigger brands also have slightly higher loyalty (S001). Growth shows mostly as penetration, with a little loyalty alongside.
