# Frameworks: Byron Sharp and the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute

14 frameworks, grouped by the job they do (buyer base, availability, memory, media, measurement). Each carries an evidence grade (A law-like, B published but narrow, C Institute framework, D argument, Inference for Moonshot use; definitions in `sources.md`). Book tags show where the idea is taught; no book text is used. Quotes are verbatim from search-page extracts of the cited source.

---

## Buyer base

### 1. Penetration-led growth *(How Brands Grow)*
- **Evidence grade:** A.
- **What it is:** Brands with bigger share mostly have more buyers, not much more loyal ones. When share changes, penetration changes most.
- **Evidence:** Across 24 datasets in 17 packaged goods categories in China, Malaysia and Indonesia, "market share change is accompanied by a greater change in penetration than in any other metric. This finding is very consistent across categories and countries." (S018). A global replication of penetration, loyalty and share (S044) and work on small brands (S043) carry the same headline. Sharp, on what readers adopted most: "you can't grow without growing the size of the customer base" (S009).
- **When to use:** Any growth plan, budget split or promotion. Ask first how many new category buyers it reaches.
- **How to score:** Estimate the share of reached or converted people who did not buy the brand in the last 12 months. A plan whose upside depends on existing buyers buying more scores low.
- **Sources:** S009, S018, S043, S044.

### 2. Double jeopardy *(How Brands Grow)*
- **Evidence grade:** A.
- **What it is:** Smaller brands have far fewer buyers, and those buyers also buy them somewhat less often. Loyalty varies far less than penetration across brands.
- **Evidence:** Ehrenberg, Goodhardt and Barwise reviewed the evidence, theories and exceptions in 1990 (S014). Later papers call it a tool that still predicts brand loyalty fifty years on (S015) and test it with extensive replication (S047). Sharp's answer to critics leans on the "double": bigger brands also have slightly higher loyalty and attitude scores (S001).
- **When to use:** When someone proposes a loyalty fix for a small brand, or sets a repeat-rate target that is out of line with brand size.
- **How to score:** Compare the brand's repeat rate to brands of similar penetration in the category. If it is in line, low loyalty is a size symptom, not a loyalty problem.
- **Sources:** S001, S014, S015, S047.

### 3. The 60/20 buyer base and light buyers
- **Evidence grade:** A for the pattern, B for any single figure.
- **What it is:** The heaviest 20% of a brand's buyers give a bit more than half its sales, not 80%. The other half comes from a long tail of light buyers, and heavy buyers tend to buy less next period.
- **Evidence:** Sharp, Romaniuk and Graham report a brand average of 59% from the heaviest 20%, and say the figure depends "on the time period, other technical decisions, brand size and some category characteristics" (S010). A 2023 study of buyer groups found 50% to 73% (S013). In the Dunnhumby dispute, Sharp and Graham said "most brand buyers are light/occasional customers yet they still account for 40% of long-run turnover" (S011).
- **When to use:** CRM-only plans, retargeting-heavy budgets, "focus on our best customers" proposals.
- **How to score:** Pull the brand's own 12-month buyer distribution. If the plan reaches mostly the top 20%, it is working on about half of sales and the half least able to grow.
- **Sources:** S010, S011, S012, S013, S040.

### 4. No brand segmentation (buyer profiles of competing brands)
- **Evidence grade:** A.
- **What it is:** Directly competing brands sell to much the same kinds of people. The practical question is how many buy, not who.
- **Evidence:** Kennedy and Ehrenberg used the UK Target Group Index, about 25,000 adults a year across 42 industries, and found competing brands' user profiles seldom differ (S035).
- **When to use:** When a brief defines the audience as "our users are X" and plans to reach only X.
- **How to score:** Compare the brand's buyer profile with its nearest rival's. If they look alike, a narrow target cuts reach without a profile advantage.
- **Sources:** S035, S036.

## Availability

### 5. Physical availability, including the digital shelf *(How Brands Grow)*
- **Evidence grade:** C for the framework; D for the search analogy; Inference for marketplaces.
- **What it is:** How easy the brand is to find and buy: presence in the places and channels category buyers use, prominence once there, and ease of purchase.
- **Evidence:** Sharp argues paid search is shelf space and says the same holds for display on Amazon and other retailer sites (S020). WARC's summary of Institute work names "digital availability" as an extension of physical availability focused on penetration growth (S021). Sharp also warns: "it is very risky to rely entirely on physical or purchase availability to build mental availability" (S020).
- **When to use:** Any marketplace, D2C or retail decision. On Shopee, Lazada and TikTok Shop, search rank, stock status, listing quality and visible price are the shelf (Inference).
- **How to score:** For the top 10 category search terms per platform, record whether the brand appears on page one, in stock, with a clear pack image. Missing slots are lost penetration.
- **Sources:** S019, S020, S021.

### 6. Generic versus specific search terms
- **Evidence grade:** D.
- **What it is:** Sharp's split for search: "the more generic the term, the more it builds mental availability for the category, and the more specific the term, the more it goes toward shelf space" (S020).
- **When to use:** Splitting marketplace keyword budgets between category words ("acne patch") and brand words ("Spiffy Patch").
- **How to score:** Brand-term spend defends existing buyers; category-term spend is where new buyers are found. A keyword plan that is mostly brand terms is mostly a loyalty plan.
- **Sources:** S020.

## Memory

### 7. Mental availability *(How Brands Grow; Better Brand Health)*
- **Evidence grade:** C.
- **What it is:** The chance a buyer notices, recognises or thinks of the brand in buying situations. It is not awareness and not liking.
- **Evidence:** Sharp: "Awareness metrics tap various bits of this (eg recall or recognition) but seldom cover the differences of different situations" (S034). He adds that mental availability is "not about what consumers like about the brand, or not" (S034).
- **When to use:** Briefs, brand tracking, and any claim that "awareness is high, so we are fine."
- **How to score:** Use the Better Brand Health metrics (framework 9), not prompted awareness.
- **Sources:** S029, S030, S034.

### 8. Category Entry Points *(How Brands Grow Part 2; Better Brand Health)*
- **Evidence grade:** C.
- **What it is:** The situations, needs and cues that make buyers think of the category. "The building blocks of mental availability are the Category Entry Points (CEPs)" (S017).
- **Parts:** Map CEPs by asking why, when, where, with whom and with what the category is bought, then rank them by how many buyers live them and how strongly the brand is already linked. This is the practitioner convention drawn from How Brands Grow Part 2 summaries, not verified against the book in this build; Romaniuk's own account of how CEPs drive growth is S016.
- **Caution from Romaniuk:** "we see many different definitions of CEP, even more interpretations, CEPs that are not CEPs" (S016). A CEP is a buying situation, not a product benefit or a tagline.
- **When to use:** Creative briefs, KOL briefs, listing titles and image copy.
- **How to score:** Count the CEPs the work links the brand to and weight each by how many category buyers face it.
- **Sources:** S016, S017.

### 9. Better Brand Health metrics *(Better Brand Health)*
- **Evidence grade:** C.
- **What it is:** Romaniuk's tracking set built on CEPs: Mental Market Share, Mental Penetration, Network Size and Share of Mind (S030). Marketing Week describes Mental Market Share as a brand's share of responses relative to competitors (S029).
- **When to use:** Replacing a brand-awareness tracker or setting brand KPIs.
- **How to score:** Mental Penetration (share of category buyers linking the brand to at least one CEP) is the first number to grow; Network Size second.
- **Sources:** S029, S030.

### 10. Distinctive asset grid (fame x uniqueness) *(Building Distinctive Brand Assets)*
- **Evidence grade:** C for the grid; B for the benchmark figures.
- **What it is:** Score each asset (colour, character, shape, logo, sound) on fame (share of category buyers who link it to the brand) and uniqueness (how exclusively they link it). Quadrants as reproduced in later research: Use or Lose (high fame, high uniqueness), Investment Potential (low fame, high uniqueness), Avoid Solo Use (high fame, low uniqueness), Ignore or Test (low on both) (S025).
- **Evidence:** A 2026 cross-industry benchmark found the average asset scores 26% fame and 54% uniqueness, and that "Uniqueness is more challenging to build than Fame" (S026). Its title reports shape-based assets as strongest (S026).
- **When to use:** Rebrands, pack changes, character licensing (Monchhichi), new creative systems.
- **How to score:** Test assets with category buyers, logo removed. Do not drop a Use or Lose asset; do not run an Avoid Solo Use asset without the brand name.
- **Sources:** S025, S026.

## Media

### 11. Reach over frequency, broad over narrow
- **Evidence grade:** C/D. The base finding (no brand segmentation, 60/20) is A; the media rule is Sharp's reasoning.
- **What it is:** Reach as many category buyers as possible at sensible cost. Sharp's rule one: "continuously reach all buyers of the category" (S004). His 2022 advice was to "reach everyone, cost effectively" (S006).
- **When to use:** Media plans, KOL rosters, platform mix.
- **How to score:** Unique category-buyer reach per baht. Penalise plans that buy high frequency on a small retargeting pool.
- **Sources:** S004, S006, S036.

### 12. Continuous presence over bursts
- **Evidence grade:** B for the cost of going dark; D for the scheduling rule.
- **What it is:** Advertising refreshes memories over time; long gaps let them fade. Sharp told marketers to spread spend over time rather than concentrate it in campaigns (S006), and argues "Today's advertising mostly affects sales in years to come (not next week)" (S039).
- **Evidence:** 41 Australian alcohol brands, 1996 to 2015: average sales change after stopping advertising was about minus 16% after one year, minus 25% after two and minus 36% after three, and "Decline was generally faster for smaller brands and brands already declining in sales prior to advertising cessation." (S027). A US replication across 365 brands in 22 categories found share falls averaging 10%, 20% and 28% after one, two and three years (S028).
- **When to use:** Mega-sale calendars (9.9, 10.10, 11.11, 12.12) that put most spend in a few days, and proposals to go quiet between campaigns.
- **How to score:** Weeks per quarter with zero brand reach. More than a few in a row is a risk, larger for a small brand (S027).
- **Sources:** S006, S027, S028, S039.

## Measurement and judgement

### 13. Sales effects in buyer terms (promotion and loyalty program checks)
- **Evidence grade:** B.
- **What it is:** Judge a promotion or loyalty program by who bought and what they did afterwards, against the Dirichlet or double jeopardy baseline.
- **Evidence:** The classic 1994 study of price-related promotions, as summarised by citing papers, found that promotion buyers were mostly existing buyers of the brand and that buying returned to its prior pattern afterwards, with no lasting gain (S024). Sharp and Sharp tested a large multi-partner loyalty program against the Dirichlet baseline and found little excess loyalty; they wrote that "loyalty programs are probably the only marketing effort which deliberately focuses on bringing about such change" (S022). Liu found a convenience-store program lifted purchases among low and moderate initial buyers over time (S023), so treat the null as typical, not universal.
- **When to use:** Every discount, voucher, bundle and membership proposal.
- **How to score:** Share of promo buyers new to the brand in 12 months; repeat rate of those new buyers versus normal new buyers; post-promo baseline.
- **Sources:** S022, S023, S024, S045.

### 14. Evidence grading and the critic's checklist
- **Evidence grade:** D (method).
- **What it is:** Sharp's demand that marketing be grounded in generalisable evidence. "Intuition and creativity are incredibly important things, but they need to be grounded in some understanding of how the world works." (S032). He attacks rules built on awards data (the 60:40 rule as "terrible, very misleading", S006) and metrics without proven sales links (attention metrics as "nonsense", S006).
- **When to use:** Any time a number or rule is quoted in the room.
- **How to score:** Ask: replicated across categories and countries? Sales data or survey? Awards or full sample? Single platform's own data? Grade A to D before acting.
- **Sources:** S006, S009, S031, S032.
