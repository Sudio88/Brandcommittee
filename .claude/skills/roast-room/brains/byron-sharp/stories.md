# Stories: Byron Sharp and the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute

15 documented studies, cases and disputes the seat reuses. Figures are traced to the study named; where only a summary was available, the entry says so.

1. **When brands go dark.** 41 alcohol brands in Australia, 20 years of spend and sales (1996 to 2015). Average sales fell about 16% after one year without advertising, 25% after two and 36% after three; "Decline was generally faster for smaller brands and brands already declining in sales prior to advertising cessation." (S027). A replication on 365 US brands in 22 categories found share falls averaging 10%, 20% and 28% (S028). Lesson: going dark is not free, and it costs small brands most. Caveat: natural experiment, not controlled.

2. **The 60/20 Pareto law.** Sharp, Romaniuk and Graham found the heaviest 20% of a brand's buyers give on average 59% of sales, not 80%, and the figure moves with time window, brand size and category (S010). A 2023 buyer-group study found 50% to 73% (S013). Lesson: half the business sits with light buyers.

3. **Sharp versus Dunnhumby on heavy buyers (2018).** Dunnhumby's report, from one UK retailer's loyalty-card data (200 million transactions, 2011 to 2016), argued heavy buyers matter more than Sharp says (S012). Sharp called heavy-buyer strategy a "dead-end" and, with Graham, replied that light and occasional buyers still give 40% of long-run turnover (S011). Lesson: a single retailer's card data is a narrow base for a law.

4. **The loyalty program test (1997).** Sharp and Sharp ran a large multi-partner program against the Dirichlet baseline and found little excess loyalty (S022). Liu later found gains among low and moderate initial buyers in a convenience-store program (S023). Lesson: expect small effects; test against a baseline.

5. **Price promotions leave no trail (1994).** Ehrenberg, Hammond and Goodhardt found promotion buyers were mostly existing buyers who returned to normal buying after the deal (S024, via citing papers). Lesson: count new-to-brand buyers before calling a promotion a success.

6. **Emerging markets: China, Malaysia, Indonesia (2018).** 24 datasets, 17 packaged goods categories. Share change came with more change in penetration than in any other metric, consistently across categories and countries, whatever the brand's price or promotional depth (S018). Lesson: the penetration law is not a Western artefact.

7. **Online versus in-store grocery (2014).** UK data on over 100 brands in 19 categories: online sales came disproportionately from the retailer's in-store buyers, brand loyalty was somewhat higher online, private label slightly stronger (S019). Lesson: online shopping is habitual; first trial matters.

8. **There is no brand segmentation (2001).** Kennedy and Ehrenberg used about 25,000 UK adults a year across 42 industries and found competing brands' buyer profiles seldom differ (S035). Lesson: ask how many, not who.

9. **Ritson's "dark ages" and Sharp's reply (2016).** After Mars CMO Bruce McColl spoke about looking beyond targeting, Ritson wrote that ditching targeting was going back to the dark ages. Sharp replied in "Don't shoot yourself with target marketing", arguing the fix for a lost audience is to broaden reach, not to target the lost group (S036). Lesson: the dispute is real and documented.

10. **The Festival of Marketing clash (2017).** Ritson and Sharp debated How Brands Grow seven years on (S046). Marketing Week reports the vote went 59% to 27% to Ritson; Ritson's column landed on "both": targeted activation plus broad brand building (S002, S005). Earlier, Ritson had defended Sharp against Marie Oldham's critique, which found half of 39 winning IPA 2016 cases showed clear targeting (S004). Millward Brown also thanked Sharp (S037). Lesson: critics who most dispute targeting still credit the laws.

11. **The 60:40 row (2022).** At a Mi3 event Sharp called Binet and Field's 60:40 rule "terrible, very misleading", said attention metrics were "nonsense", and urged always-on reach (S006). Field called it "tacky self-publicising" (S007). Binet has said "we agree on much more than we disagree" (S008). Lesson: the disagreement is about rules of thumb and data sources, not about reach or brand building.

12. **"Byron Sharp bites back" at the IPA.** Sharp said many marketers misread How Brands Grow; he never claimed loyalty did not exist (S031). Lesson: correct the misreadings before debating.

13. **Paid search is shelf space (2023).** Sharp's LinkedIn piece and comment thread: search and retailer-site display put the brand in front of people already in the market, with generic terms building more category memory than specific ones (S020). Lesson: marketplace search is availability.

14. **Distinctive asset benchmarks (2026).** A cross-industry study found the average asset at 26% fame and 54% uniqueness, uniqueness harder to build, and shape-based assets strongest (S026). Lesson: most assets are weaker than brand teams believe; test them.

15. **CEPs in a personal care case (2023).** A case study on Romaniuk's site used category entry points to find where a personal care brand could raise mental market share (S017). Lesson: CEP mapping is a working tool, and personal care is close to Moonshot's categories.
