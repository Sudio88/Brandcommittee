# Positions: Byron Sharp and the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute

Stances on the decision types that recur in the Moonshot Roast Room, then a section applying the evidence to marketplace and D2C brands in emerging markets. Each stance has an evidence grade, verbatim quotes with source id and date where the extract allowed, and the documented counterpoint. "Inference" marks this seat's application to Moonshot; it is not an Ehrenberg-Bass finding.

## Recurring decisions

**1. Discount promotions (vouchers, flash sales, mega-sale days, bundles).** Grade B.
Price promotions mostly reach people who already buy the brand, at a lower price, and buying returns to normal afterwards (S024, 1994, as summarised by citing papers). Penetration-led share change held regardless of a brand's initial price or promotional depth in the emerging-market study: "pricing strategy (initial price and promotional depth) did not alter the main pattern of results" (S018, 2018).
- Stance: Promotions are a cost of being on the shelf on marketplace sale days, not a growth strategy. Check who bought: if most promo buyers already bought the brand, the promo bought volume at a lower margin, not growth.
- Inference for Moonshot: on Shopee and Lazada, joining 11.11 can be a physical availability decision (being in the sale feed and search results that day). Judge it on new-to-brand buyers and their later repeat, not on GMV.
- Counterpoint: later studies using finer analysis have reported some negative purchase feedback after promotions (noted in the literature citing S024), so the null is the typical case, not a law. No documented Ritson or Binet dispute on this point was found.

**2. Loyalty programs, memberships, CRM-led growth.** Grade B.
Sharp and Sharp found a large multi-partner program produced little excess loyalty against the Dirichlet baseline; "loyalty programs are probably the only marketing effort which deliberately focuses on bringing about such change" (S022, 1997). Sharp later criticised a study he called misleading on program effects (S045, 2008).
- Stance: Fine as a convenience or a cost of doing business; do not fund one as the growth engine, especially for a small brand where double jeopardy caps loyalty.
- Counterpoint: Liu (S023, 2007) found gains among low and moderate initial buyers in a convenience-store program. Treat a modest lift among light buyers as possible, not a step change.

**3. Targeting and segmentation.** Grade A for the buyer-profile finding; D for the media rule.
Competing brands' buyers seldom differ in profile (S035, 2001). Sharp's rule one: "continuously reach all buyers of the category" (S004, quoted 2017). When a brand loses young buyers, "the solution is to reverse the failed strategy and broaden the brand's reach" (S036, 2016-04-27).
- Stance: Define the audience as category buyers. Targeting that excludes most category buyers is the risk; targeting media at category buyers rather than at the whole population is consistent with the evidence.
- Counterpoint (documented dispute): Mark Ritson argued that "Ditching targeting for mass marketing is going back to the dark ages" (column title, quoted in S036) and that "it is much more cost-effective to speak to a mass market" for brand building, but "none of you have got enough money to do it" (S002). His resolution is both: targeted activation plus broad brand building (S002). At the 2017 Festival of Marketing debate, Marketing Week coverage reports an audience vote of 59% to 27% for Ritson (S005, S002). Marie Oldham found half of the 39 winning IPA 2016 cases showed clear targeting (reported in S004).

**4. Brand versus performance (budget split).** Grade D, contested.
Sharp called Binet and Field's 60:40 rule "terrible, very misleading" and said it was coined because people "wanted a number" (S006, 2022-08-23). He argues brands need both activation and advertising, but that without memory-building advertising no amount of performance spend delivers growth (S009 summary, 2020).
- Stance: Do not set the split by a ratio. Set it by job: physical availability (search, listings, marketplace ads) always on; reach-based advertising to all category buyers always on; neither starved.
- Counterpoint (documented dispute): Binet's own framing, as reported alongside Sharp's talk, is that the ratio is "not an absolute" and may vary by category (S006), and "we agree on much more than we disagree"; "journalists often focus on our differences because it makes for a better story" (S008). Peter Field called Sharp's comments "tacky self-publicising" (S007, 2022-09-07).

**5. Creative consistency and distinctive assets.** Grade C, B benchmarks.
Score assets on fame and uniqueness (S025). Average assets reach 26% fame and 54% uniqueness, and "Uniqueness is more challenging to build than Fame" (S026, 2026). Sharp is sceptical of producing many creative executions; he suggested multiple iterations of creative could hinder growth (S006 report, 2022).
- Stance: Keep a small set of assets and use them every time, early in each video and on every listing image. Change campaigns, not the brand code. Retire an asset only after testing shows it is weak.
- Inference for Moonshot: the Monchhichi character is likely high fame in Thailand but must be tested for uniqueness to Moonshot's licensed goods; The Spiffy Patch needs a shape or colour asset that reads in a 1-second scroll.
- Counterpoint: Ritson credits Sharp's focus on distinctive assets over mission and purpose (S004), so there is little dispute here.

**6. New brand launch (for example a new Linee or Loom line).** Grade A for the pattern; Inference for tactics.
A new brand starts small, so double jeopardy applies: few buyers, slightly lower loyalty (S014). Growth will show as penetration (S018). Smaller brands lose fastest when advertising stops (S027).
- Stance: Launch for reach and availability, not for a loyal niche. Get listed and findable on every channel category buyers use, link the brand to the two or three biggest CEPs, set distinctive assets from day one, and keep reach continuous after launch week.
- Do not judge a launch on repeat rate in month one; judge it on how many category buyers tried it and whether repeat matches what brand size predicts (S014).

**7. E-commerce and digital availability.** Grade D for the shelf analogy; B for online buying patterns; Inference for marketplaces.
"the more generic the term, the more it builds mental availability for the category, and the more specific the term, the more it goes toward shelf space" (S020, 2023). "it is very risky to rely entirely on physical or purchase availability to build mental availability" (S020). Online grocery in the UK showed the same brand patterns with slightly higher loyalty (S019, 2014).
- Stance: Marketplace search results, sponsored slots, stock status and listing quality are physical availability. Keep them always on. Pair them with reach advertising, because shelf space alone does not build memory.

**8. Purpose-led and attention-metric briefs.** Grade D.
An Institute headline reports Sharp warning that purpose could be "the death of brands" (S038, title). He called attention metrics "nonsense" (S006).
- Stance: Ask for the sales evidence before adopting either as a KPI.

## Applying the evidence to marketplace and D2C brands in emerging markets

What the Institute has published that is closest to Moonshot's situation, and what is this seat's inference.

**What the evidence says (graded).**
- **Penetration drives share in Southeast and East Asia too (B, close to A).** 24 datasets, 17 packaged goods categories, China, Malaysia and Indonesia: "market share change is accompanied by a greater change in penetration than in any other metric. This finding is very consistent across categories and countries." (S018). Of the loyalty measures, share of category requirements weighed more in Indonesia and purchase frequency in Malaysia (S018).
- **Price level and promo depth do not change that pattern (B).** "pricing strategy (initial price and promotional depth) did not alter the main pattern of results" (S018).
- **Online channels follow the same laws (B).** Online grocery sales drew mostly on the retailer's existing in-store buyers, with slightly higher brand loyalty online (S019). Expect marketplace buyers to be habitual and to repeat what they bought before, which favours brands that win the first trial.
- **Search and retailer display are shelf space (D).** Sharp extends the logic to retailer websites (S020); WARC's summary calls it digital availability (S021).
- **Gap:** no Ehrenberg-Bass study of Thailand, of Shopee, Lazada or TikTok Shop, of live commerce, or of KOL selling was found in this build. Anything on those is Inference.

**What this seat infers for Moonshot (Inference, say so in the room).**
1. **The marketplace search shelf is the first job.** For each brand, own page one on the top category terms in Thai and English on Shopee, Lazada and TikTok Shop. Stock-outs and delisted SKUs are lost penetration.
2. **Split keyword money by job.** Category terms find new buyers; brand terms defend existing ones (S020). A plan that is mostly brand-term spend is a loyalty plan.
3. **Live and KOL as reach, judged on new buyers.** Treat KOL and live sessions as media that reach category buyers. Brief creators on CEPs and on showing the distinctive assets early, and judge on reach among non-buyers and new-to-brand orders, not GMV from existing followers.
4. **Mega-sale days are shelf presence, not strategy.** Be in the sale feed, but keep reach running in the weeks between 9.9, 10.10, 11.11 and 12.12 (S027 shows the cost of going dark).
5. **D2C is a small shelf.** Most category buyers shop on marketplaces; D2C serves existing buyers and margin. Do not move budget from marketplace availability to D2C CRM in the name of loyalty (S010, S022).
6. **Habit online raises the value of first trial.** If online buyers repeat what they bought before (S019), early penetration on marketplaces compounds. This favours getting into baskets now over margin-perfect launches later.
